## PAGE 1: What is data?
Data is information that we collect. It can be numbers, names or counts. When the teacher counts how many pupils are present, that count is data. When a shopkeeper writes how many packets of sugar were sold today, that is data too. When a farmer notes how many eggs the hens laid, that is data.

We collect data to answer questions. How many pupils walk to school? Which fruit do most children like? How many vehicles pass the school gate? Once we have collected the data, we can put it in order, look at it and use it to make decisions.

### Raw data
Data that has just been collected and has not yet been arranged is called raw data. Raw data can look untidy. For example, ten pupils are asked their favourite fruit and their answers are written as they come: mango, banana, mango, orange, banana, mango, pawpaw, mango, banana, orange.

### Why organise data?
Raw data is hard to read and easy to get wrong. If we put it in a table, it becomes clear and easy to answer questions from.

### Think about it
List three questions that a school could answer by collecting data. For example: How many pupils are in each class? Which games do pupils like most? How many books are in the library?

## PAGE 2: Materials for collecting and recording data
Before collecting data, we need to get ready. The right materials make our work neat and accurate.

### Things we may need
• A pencil or pen and an eraser, for writing and correcting.
• An exercise book, a sheet of paper or a chart, for recording.
• A ruler, for drawing straight lines to make a table.
• A clipboard or a hard book, to write on while standing.
• A clock or a watch, if we are counting for a set time.
• A tally chart, to record counts quickly.
• A digital device such as a phone or tablet, if an adult allows us to use one.

### Planning before you collect
Step 1: Decide the question you want to answer, for example, "What types of vehicles pass the school gate?"
Step 2: Decide who or what you will count and where you will do it.
Step 3: Decide how long you will collect. For example, 10 minutes.
Step 4: Prepare a page with the names of the things you expect to count.
Step 5: Work safely, in a group, with an adult or a teacher nearby.

### Honesty
Record only what you really see or hear. Do not make up numbers. Honest data helps everyone make good decisions.

### Working together
In a group, share the tasks. One person watches, one person records and one checks. Take turns so that everyone gets a chance.

## PAGE 3: Collecting data
There are several ways to collect data.
• Observing: looking and counting, for example counting vehicles that pass.
• Asking questions: asking pupils about their favourite food or game.
• Counting objects: counting desks, books or trees in the school compound.
• Measuring: measuring the height of pupils or the length of leaves.

### A small collection: types of vehicles
Ten vehicles were seen passing the school gate. A group wrote them down in order: matatu, boda boda, matatu, bus, matatu, boda boda, lorry, matatu, matatu, bus.

That is 10 items. Raw data like this is hard to use. Which type was seen most? We need to count each type.

### Keep the collection small
In this grade we collect at most 10 items at a time, so that counting is easy and correct. If you see 10 vehicles, stop and check your record.

### Safety while collecting data
Never stand in or near the road while counting vehicles. Do your counting from a safe place inside the school gate, with your teacher. Take care near roads. If someone is hurt, tell an adult at once. In Kenya you can call 999 or 112 for emergency help, and the type of vehicles that use a road helps people know which help might be needed, for example an ambulance.

### Reading the tally chart
• Matatu has one group of five: 5.
• Boda boda has 2 tallies: 2.
• Bus has 2 tallies: 2.
• Lorry has 1 tally: 1.
Add the totals: 5 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 10. This matches the 10 vehicles we saw, so our count is correct.

### Counting tallies
Count each group of 5 as five, then add the extra strokes. Six is one group of five and one stroke.

### Tip
Make a tally mark as soon as you see each item, and make only one mark per item. If you are not sure, ask your group so that you do not count an item twice.

## PAGE 5: Drawing a table
A table is a neat way of showing data. It has rows and columns. A row goes across the page, and a column goes down. A good table has a title, headings for each column, and a place for the totals.

### Steps for drawing a table
Step 1: Use a ruler to draw a rectangle on your page.
Step 2: Write a title above the table, for example "Favourite fruits of 10 pupils".
Step 3: Draw a vertical line to make two columns. Label them "Fruit" and "Number of pupils".
Step 4: Draw a horizontal line under the headings, and a line for each fruit and for the total.
Step 5: Write the names of the fruits in the first column.

### Advantages of a table
It puts the information in order, it is easy to read and it shows the totals. Each kind of item has its own row.

### Try it
Ask 10 friends which colour they like best. Make a tally chart, then draw a table with the totals.

## PAGE 6: Interpreting data from a table
Interpreting data means reading a table and finding out what it tells us. We answer questions using the numbers.

Use the fruit table: mango 4, banana 3, orange 2, pawpaw 1.

### Questions and answers
• Which fruit is the most popular? Mango, because 4 is the biggest number.
• Which is the least popular? Pawpaw, because 1 is the smallest number.
• How many pupils were asked? Add all: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 pupils.
• How many more pupils like mango than orange? 4 − 2 = 2 pupils.
• How many pupils like banana or orange? 3 + 2 = 5 pupils.
• How many pupils did not choose mango? 10 − 4 = 6 pupils.

### Using the data to decide
A school shop wants to buy fruit. Mango is chosen most, so the shop should buy more mangoes than pawpaws. Data helps us to make good decisions and to share resources fairly.

### Vehicle data
In the vehicle table, matatus were the most common with 5 out of 10. Boda bodas and buses were 2 each. Only 1 lorry passed. This tells us that most of the traffic at that time was public passenger vehicles, so pupils need to be very careful when crossing.

### Write sentences
Practise writing a sentence about a table. "Out of 10 vehicles, 5 were matatus." This is a clear statement that anyone can check.

### Data checklist
1. Decide the question.
2. Collect at most 10 items, using a tally chart.
3. Draw a table with a title and headings.
4. Write the totals and check they add up.
5. Read the table and answer questions.

### Common mistakes
• Counting an item twice or missing one.
• Forgetting the title or the headings.
• Not adding up the total to check.
• Mixing up the numbers, for example writing 5 in the mango row when the tally shows 4.

### Summary
• Data is information we collect.
• A tally chart helps us count quickly, in groups of five.
• A table has a title, headings, rows and columns.
• We interpret data by reading the table and answering questions.
• Data helps us make sensible decisions.

## QUIZ
1. What is data?
   A) Information that we collect
   B) A kind of shape
   C) A unit of time
   D) A kind of measuring tape
   Answer: A
   Why: Data is information, such as counts, names or numbers, that we collect to answer questions.
2. How many items are shown by one group of tally marks with four upright lines crossed by a fifth line?
   A) 4
   B) 5
   C) 6
   D) 10
   Answer: B
   Why: Four upright strokes crossed by a fifth make a group of 5.
3. In a table of favourite fruits, mango has 4, banana 3, orange 2 and pawpaw 1. Which fruit is the most popular?
   A) Banana
   B) Orange
   C) Mango
   D) Pawpaw
   Answer: C
   Why: Mango has the biggest number, 4.
4. Using the same table, how many pupils were asked altogether?
   A) 4
   B) 6
   C) 9
   D) 10
   Answer: D
   Why: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 pupils.
5. What should every good table have?
   A) A title and column headings
   B) A picture only
   C) No numbers
   D) A long story
   Answer: A
   Why: A title tells what the table is about and headings tell what each column shows.
6. Ten vehicles passed: 5 matatus, 2 boda bodas, 2 buses and 1 lorry. Draw a table to show this data. (3 marks)
   Answer: A table with the title and two column headings, Vehicle and Number, with rows matatu 5, boda boda 2, bus 2, lorry 1 and total 10.
   Why: 1 mark for a title and headings, 1 mark for all four rows correct, 1 mark for the total 10.
7. Using the vehicle data in question 6, which type of vehicle was seen most? How many more matatus than buses were seen? (3 marks)
   Answer: Matatus were seen most. There were 5 − 2 = 3 more matatus than buses.
   Why: 1 mark for matatu, 1 mark for the subtraction 5 − 2, 1 mark for the answer 3.
8. Give two things you should do to stay safe when you collect data about vehicles. (2 marks)
   Answer: Count from a safe place inside the school gate and stay with a teacher or an adult. Do not stand in or near the road.
   Why: 1 mark for each sensible safety rule.
9. In a tally chart, a group of five tallies and 3 more tally marks show which number? Explain how you count. (2 marks)
   Answer: 8. Count the group of five as 5 and then add 3 more, so 5 + 3 = 8.
   Why: 1 mark for 8 and 1 mark for the explanation.
10. Ten pupils chose their favourite drink: 4 chose juice, 3 chose milk, 2 chose water and 1 chose tea. Write two facts that you can read from this data. (2 marks)
   Answer: For example, juice is the most popular drink with 4 pupils. Only 1 pupil chose tea. 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 pupils were asked.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct fact based on the numbers.
