## PAGE 1: What is capacity?
Capacity is the amount of liquid that a container can hold when it is full. A water bottle, a bucket, a jerrycan, a cooking pot and a tank all have capacity. A big bucket has a bigger capacity than a small cup.

Liquids such as water, milk, juice, cooking oil and paraffin take the shape of the container that holds them. So we measure them by the capacity of the container.

### The litre
The unit that we use to measure capacity is the litre. We write it as L. A packet of milk is often 1 litre. A big jerrycan may hold 20 litres. A water tank at a school may hold thousands of litres.

Some containers are larger than 1 litre and some are smaller. A cup holds much less than 1 litre. A bucket holds many litres.

### Which unit?
• Use litres for drinks, water in a bucket, fuel, oil and milk.
• Use bigger numbers of litres for tanks and drums.

### Think about it
Can you name three containers at home that hold more than 1 litre? A bucket, a jerrycan and a large cooking pot are good examples. Can you name two that hold less than 1 litre? A cup and a small spoon are examples.

### Counting the pours
Suppose you fill a bucket using a 1 litre cup. You pour 8 cups and the bucket is full. The bucket has a capacity of 8 litres.

### Reading the scale
On a measuring jug, find the line that the water reaches. The number on that line is the amount of water in litres. Look at the jug from the side, at the same height as the water, so that you read the correct line.

### Try it
Use a 1 litre bottle to find the capacity of a small basin. Write how many bottles you used. That number of litres is the capacity of the basin.

## PAGE 3: Half a litre and a quarter of a litre
Many containers hold less than one litre, or a part of a litre. Two very common parts are half a litre and a quarter of a litre.

Half a litre is written 1/2 L. Two half-litre containers fill 1 litre. A quarter of a litre is written 1/4 L. Four quarter-litre containers fill 1 litre. Also, two quarters make one half.

### Making the containers
To make a half-litre container, pour 1 litre of water into a clean bottle. Pour half of it into a second bottle so that both bottles have the same level. Mark the water level on the second bottle. That mark shows 1/2 L. Do the same with four equal bottles to make 1/4 L marks.

### Combining
• 1/2 L + 1/2 L = 1 L
• 1/4 L + 1/4 L = 1/2 L
• 1/4 L + 1/4 L + 1/4 L = 3/4 L
• 1 L + 1/2 L = 1 1/2 L

### Safety
Be careful with water on the floor and never taste any liquid you are measuring. Some liquids, such as paraffin, are not safe to drink or touch.

## PAGE 4: Making litre containers from local materials
You do not need shops to make measures. You can use plastic bottles and containers you find at home. This also helps to keep the environment clean.

A 1 litre container is the standard. You can find out whether a bottle holds 1 litre by comparing it with a litre packet of milk or a bottle labelled 1 litre.

### Filling and emptying method
Step 1: Take a container marked 1 L, such as a bottle or a packet of juice.
Step 2: Fill it with clean water.
Step 3: Pour it into an unmarked container, such as a bucket.
Step 4: Draw a line at the water level on the bucket, and write 1 L next to it.
Step 5: Add another litre and mark 2 L. Continue until you reach the top.

### Testing a homemade measure
Ask a friend to pour a litre of water into your homemade measure. If the water reaches your 1 L line, your measure is correct. If it does not, adjust the line.

### Using bigger containers
A 5 litre jerrycan can be filled by 5 pours of a 1 litre bottle. A 20 litre jerrycan needs 20 such pours, or 4 pours of a 5 litre container. Notice that 4 × 5 = 20.

## PAGE 5: Adding capacity
We can add capacities to find how much liquid there is altogether. Add the whole litres first, and then add the parts of a litre.

### Whole litres
A farmer fills one drum with 12 L and another with 8 L. Total = 12 + 8 = 20 L.

### Litres and parts of a litre
Add 1 1/4 L and 2 1/2 L.
Step 1: Add the whole litres: 1 + 2 = 3 L.
Step 2: Add the parts. 1/4 + 1/2. Since 1/2 = 2/4, we have 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4 L.
Step 3: Put them together: 3 L + 3/4 L = 3 3/4 L.

### Another example
Wanjiku has 2 L of milk. She buys 1 1/2 L more. Total = 2 + 1 1/2 = 3 1/2 L.

### Check
If you pour 1 1/4 L and 2 1/2 L into one jug, the water reaches the 3 3/4 L mark.

## PAGE 6: Subtracting capacity
Subtraction of capacity tells us how much is left, or how much more is needed. Subtract the whole litres first, then the fractions of a litre. If the fraction to be subtracted is bigger, change one whole litre into parts.

### Whole litres
A tank has 50 L of water. The family uses 18 L. Water left: 50 − 18 = 32 L.

### Litres and parts of a litre
A jerrycan holds 5 L. Musa pours out 2 1/2 L. How much is left?
5 − 2 1/2: take away 2 L to get 3 L, then take away another 1/2 L: 3 − 1/2 = 2 1/2 L.

### A harder one
Work out 4 L − 1 3/4 L.
Step 1: 4 L is not big enough in quarters to take 3/4 away, so take one whole litre and change it into 4/4. Now 4 L = 3 4/4 L.
Step 2: 3 4/4 − 1 3/4: the wholes give 3 − 1 = 2 and the quarters give 4/4 − 3/4 = 1/4. The answer is 2 1/4.
The answer is 2 1/4 L.

### Checking
Add the answer back: 2 1/4 + 1 3/4 = 4 L. The answer matches.

### Word problem
A bucket holds 10 L. A boy uses a 2 L can to fill it. He has poured 3 cans. How many more litres are needed? 3 × 2 = 6 L poured. 10 − 6 = 4 L more.

## PAGE 7: Capacity problems and summary
Here are some problems that use everything we have learned.

### Problem 1
A 20 L jerrycan is used to fill 5 L buckets. How many buckets can be filled? 20 ÷ 5 = 4 buckets.

### Problem 2
A shop sells 3 packets of milk of 1/2 L each and 2 packets of 1 L each. How many litres altogether? 3 × 1/2 L = 1 1/2 L. 2 × 1 L = 2 L. Total = 1 1/2 + 2 = 3 1/2 L.

### Problem 3
Amina has a 3 L jug. She pours 1 1/4 L into a pot and 1/2 L into a cup. How much is left in the jug?
Total used = 1 1/4 + 1/2 = 1 3/4 L. Left = 3 − 1 3/4 = 1 1/4 L.

### Summary
• Capacity is the amount a container can hold.
• The litre (L) is the unit of capacity.
• 1/2 L is half of a litre and 1/4 L is a quarter of a litre.
• To add or subtract capacity, work with the whole litres first and then the parts.
• Always check by doing the opposite operation.

### Common mistakes
• Reading the scale from above instead of level with the water.
• Forgetting that 1/2 L is the same as 2/4 L.
• Not changing a litre into quarters when subtracting bigger fractions.

## QUIZ
1. What is capacity?
   A) The amount of liquid a container can hold
   B) The height of a container
   C) The mass of a container
   D) The colour of a container
   Answer: A
   Why: Capacity is how much liquid a container holds when it is full.
2. How many quarter-litre containers fill 1 litre?
   A) 2
   B) 3
   C) 4
   D) 8
   Answer: C
   Why: Four quarters make one whole, so four 1/4 L containers make 1 L.
3. What is 1 1/4 L + 2 1/2 L?
   A) 3 1/4 L
   B) 3 1/2 L
   C) 3 3/4 L
   D) 4 L
   Answer: C
   Why: Whole litres: 1 + 2 = 3. Parts: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. The total is 3 3/4 L.
4. A jerrycan holds 5 L. If 2 1/2 L is poured out, how much is left?
   A) 2 L
   B) 2 1/2 L
   C) 3 L
   D) 7 1/2 L
   Answer: B
   Why: 5 − 2 1/2 = 2 1/2 L.
5. How many 5 L buckets can be filled from a 20 L jerrycan?
   A) 2
   B) 4
   C) 5
   D) 15
   Answer: B
   Why: 20 ÷ 5 = 4 buckets.
6. Work out 4 L − 1 3/4 L. Show your working. (3 marks)
   Answer: 2 1/4 L
   Why: Change 4 L to 3 4/4 L (1 mark). 3 4/4 − 1 3/4 = 2 1/4 (1 mark). Check: 2 1/4 + 1 3/4 = 4 L (1 mark).
7. Wanjiku has 2 L of milk and buys 1 1/2 L more. How much milk does she have now? (2 marks)
   Answer: 3 1/2 L
   Why: 2 + 1 1/2 = 3 1/2. 1 mark for the addition and 1 mark for the unit.
8. A tank has 50 L of water. The family uses 18 L. How much water is left? (2 marks)
   Answer: 32 L
   Why: 50 − 18 = 32. 1 mark for the subtraction and 1 mark for the answer with units.
9. A shop sells 3 packets of milk of 1/2 L each and 2 packets of 1 L each. How many litres of milk is that altogether? (3 marks)
   Answer: 3 1/2 L
   Why: 3 × 1/2 L = 1 1/2 L (1 mark). 2 × 1 L = 2 L (1 mark). 1 1/2 + 2 = 3 1/2 L (1 mark).
10. Describe how to make a 1/2 L mark on an unmarked bottle using a 1 L container. (3 marks)
   Answer: Pour 1 L of water into one bottle. Pour half of it into a second identical bottle so both levels match. Mark the level on the second bottle as 1/2 L.
   Why: 1 mark for starting with a 1 L amount, 1 mark for sharing it equally into two bottles, 1 mark for marking the level as 1/2 L.
