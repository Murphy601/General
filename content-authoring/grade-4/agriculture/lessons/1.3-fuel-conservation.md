## PAGE 1: What is a fuel?
A fuel is something we burn to get heat. We use the heat to cook food, boil water, warm a room or run an engine. Every home needs some kind of fuel for cooking.

Fuels do not last forever. When we cut trees for firewood and charcoal, forests shrink. Gas and kerosene come from under the ground and have to be bought with money. If we waste fuel, we spend more money and we harm the environment. To conserve fuel means to use only what we need and to make it last longer.

### Fuels used at home
• Firewood: dry pieces of wood, used on a three-stone fire or in a jiko.
• Charcoal: black lumps made by burning wood slowly with little air. It is used in a charcoal jiko.
• Gas (LPG): liquid gas kept in a metal cylinder, used in a gas cooker.
• Kerosene: a liquid fuel used in a stove or a lamp.

Some homes also use electricity, biogas made from animal dung, or the sun for cooking. In this lesson we focus on firewood, charcoal, gas and kerosene.

### Think about it
Which fuel is used for cooking in your home? Where does it come from, and how does your family get it?

## PAGE 2: Comparing home fuels
Each fuel has good points and things to watch out for. Knowing them helps us to use each one wisely and safely.

### Firewood
Dry wood burns well. Wet wood makes a lot of smoke and gives little heat. A three-stone fire loses a lot of heat to the air. Smoke can hurt the eyes and lungs. Cutting many trees leads to deforestation.

### Charcoal
Charcoal burns with little smoke and gives steady heat. It is made from trees, so heavy use also cuts down forests. Never burn charcoal inside a closed room. It gives off a poisonous gas that you cannot see or smell, and people can fall ill.

### Gas
Gas heats quickly and cleanly, and it is easy to switch off. The cylinder and cooker must be handled carefully. If you smell gas, do not light a match. Close the cylinder valve, open doors and windows, and call an adult.

### Kerosene
Kerosene is a liquid that catches fire easily. Keep it in a labelled container with a tight cap, away from children and away from fire. Never pour kerosene onto a fire, and never fill a stove while it is burning.

### Remember
Fuels can burn us. Children should cook only with an adult nearby. Keep fuels out of reach of young children.

## PAGE 3: Ways of saving fuel when cooking
There are many easy ways to save fuel, and they cost little or nothing. The main idea is to keep the heat where the food is and to stop burning when the cooking is finished.

### Practical ways
• Prepare everything before lighting the fire. Wash, peel and cut the food first so the fire does not burn while you look for things.
• Put a lid on the pot. Steam and heat stay inside, so food cooks faster.
• Use a pot the right size for the fire and the amount of food.
• Cut food into small pieces. Small pieces cook faster than big ones.
• Soak dry beans or maize in water for some hours before cooking. They cook in less time.
• When water starts to boil, turn the flame down or move wood back. It does not need to be big to keep boiling.
• Use dry firewood. Wet wood wastes heat drying itself.
• Put out the fire or close the gas valve as soon as you have finished. Never leave a fire burning with nothing on it.
• Cook several foods together where you can. For example, use the hot water for tea after cooking.

### Activity
Cook a small pot of water with the lid on and another of the same size with the lid off, on fires of the same size. Which boils first? The adult in charge should do the cooking while you observe and time it.

### Types of fuel-saving equipment
• Improved jiko (fuel-efficient jiko): its walls hold heat around the pot, so it uses less charcoal or wood than an open fire.
• Firewood stoves with a chimney: they use less wood and take smoke out of the room.
• Gas cooker with a tight-fitting pot: the flame is only as big as the pot.
• Thermos or insulated flask: keeps boiled water hot so you do not boil again.
• Pressure cookers cook food faster. They must be used by adults, because steam under pressure is dangerous.

### Making your own
A simple way of saving fuel is to build a fireplace with a wall of clay or bricks around the pot, leaving a small door for the wood and a gap for air. This keeps wind from blowing the heat away. An adult should help build it.

## PAGE 5: Why conserving fuel matters
When we save fuel, we protect trees and forests, we save money and time, and we cause less smoke. Trees keep soil in place, give shade, give us fruit, and hold water. Forests are also home to many animals and birds.

### Benefits of saving fuel
• Fewer trees are cut, so forests and water sources are protected.
• The family spends less money on gas, charcoal or kerosene.
• Less time is spent looking for firewood, so children have more time to study and play.
• Less smoke means healthier eyes and lungs.
• Fewer fires means fewer accidents.

### Planting trees for fuel
A family can plant fast-growing trees on the shamba for firewood. One tree cut should be replaced with two or more new ones. This is one way to make sure we do not run out.

### Fire safety rules
• Keep children away from fires and hot stoves.
• Keep flammable things such as paper, cloth and kerosene away from the fire.
• Never leave fire unattended.
• Put out fire completely with water or by closing the gas valve.
• If clothes catch fire, stop, drop to the ground and roll. Tell an adult at once.

### Share your ideas
In groups, make a short poster or talk on three ways your class can help to save fuel at school or home. Tell the class your ideas.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
A fuel gives heat, and fuels can run out. We save fuel by cooking with lids, cutting food small, soaking hard foods, using dry wood, using efficient stoves and putting out fires when done.

### Remember
• Firewood, charcoal, gas and kerosene are common fuels at home.
• Never use charcoal in a closed room.
• Keep kerosene and gas away from fire and from young children.
• Save fuel to protect trees, money and health.

### Common mistakes
• Cooking without a lid.
• Using wet firewood.
• Leaving the fire burning after cooking.
• Using a big flame under a small pot, so most heat is wasted.
• Not preparing food first, so the fire burns while you wait.

### Quick check
A mother has boiled beans that took a long time. What could she do next time to save fuel? She could soak the beans first, cut any large pieces small, and cook them with a lid.

## QUIZ
1. Which of these is a fuel used at home?
   A) Charcoal
   B) Sand
   C) Stone
   D) Water
   Answer: A
   Why: Charcoal burns to give heat. Sand, stone and water do not burn.
2. Why does putting a lid on a cooking pot save fuel?
   A) It makes the pot heavier
   B) It keeps the heat and steam inside so food cooks faster
   C) It makes the fire hotter
   D) It cleans the food
   Answer: B
   Why: The lid stops heat from escaping, so the food cooks in less time and less fuel is used.
3. What should you do when you have finished cooking on a gas cooker?
   A) Leave the gas on for later
   B) Close the gas valve and switch off the burner
   C) Pour water on the cylinder
   D) Cover the cooker with a cloth
   Answer: B
   Why: Closing the valve stops fuel being wasted and prevents gas leaks and fires.
4. Why should charcoal never be burnt in a closed room?
   A) It becomes too hot
   B) It produces a poisonous gas that can make people ill
   C) It uses too much oxygen from the food
   D) It makes the room dirty
   Answer: B
   Why: Burning charcoal gives off a poisonous gas that cannot be seen or smelt, so rooms need fresh air.
5. Name four types of fuel used for cooking at home. (2 marks)
   Answer: Firewood, charcoal, gas and kerosene.
   Why: Half a mark for each correct fuel, total 2 marks for four.
6. Give three ways of saving fuel while cooking. (3 marks)
   Answer: Use a lid on the pot, cut food into small pieces, put out the fire after cooking. Also accept soaking beans, using dry firewood or an improved jiko.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct way.
7. A family in a village cuts many trees for firewood. Explain two problems this can cause. (2 marks)
   Answer: Forests will shrink, and the soil will be washed away more easily. They will also have to walk farther to find wood.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct problem.
8. Explain how an improved jiko saves fuel. (2 marks)
   Answer: Its walls keep the heat close to the pot, so less charcoal or wood is needed to cook the food.
   Why: 1 mark for keeping heat near the pot, 1 mark for less fuel needed.
9. List three safety rules to follow when using fuels at home. (3 marks)
   Answer: Keep fuels away from young children; keep kerosene in a labelled container away from fire; never leave a fire burning unattended.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct rule.
