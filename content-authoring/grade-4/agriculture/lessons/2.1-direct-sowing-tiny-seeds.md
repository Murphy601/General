## PAGE 1: What is direct sowing?
Some crops are first grown in a small nursery bed and later moved to the field. This is called transplanting. Other crops are sown straight into the place where they will grow until harvest. This is called direct sowing.

Sowing means putting seeds into the soil so that they can grow. In direct sowing the seed is sown where the plant will stay. The seedlings are never moved.

### Crops with tiny seeds sown directly
• Carrots: the orange root is the food. Moving a young carrot damages its root and makes it grow crooked, so carrots are sown directly.
• Millet: a cereal grown for grain, used for porridge and ugali.
• Amaranth (terere): a leafy vegetable.
• Radish: a small round root that grows quickly.
• Sorghum: a cereal grown in dry areas.

Not all seeds are tiny. Maize and bean seeds are big. Cabbage and tomato seeds are small too but these crops are usually first grown in a nursery and then transplanted.

### Why sow directly?
• Roots are not disturbed, so the plants grow well.
• There is no need for a nursery.
• It saves time and work.
• Crops with roots as food, like carrots, grow straight.

### Think about it
Look at a carrot seed or a few millet seeds. How big is one seed compared with a bean or maize seed?

## PAGE 2: Preparing a fine seedbed
A seedbed is the piece of ground where seeds are sown. Tiny seeds have very little food stored inside, so they cannot push through hard lumps of soil. The seedbed must be fine, level and free of weeds.

### Steps for preparing the seedbed
1. Choose a place with plenty of sunshine, near water, and without standing water.
2. Clear the weeds, stones and old roots. Remove sticks and rubbish.
3. Dig the soil with a jembe or fork. Turn it over and loosen it to the depth of a hand or more.
4. Break the lumps with the back of a rake or hoe until the soil is fine and crumbly, like brown sugar.
5. Mix in a spade of compost or well-rotted manure, and mix it in well.
6. Level the surface with a rake so that water does not collect in one spot.
7. Water lightly the day before sowing so that the soil is damp but not muddy.

### Container seedbed
If you do not have a garden, use a container: a wooden box, a large tin, a basin or a plastic tub. Make small holes at the bottom so extra water can drain out. Fill it with fine soil mixed with compost, level it and use it in the same way as a garden seedbed.

### Tools
• Jembe or hoe: for digging and breaking soil.
• Rake: for making the soil fine and level.
• Watering can with a fine rose: for gentle watering.
• Stick or ruler: for making straight rows.

### Steps for sowing
1. Use a stick to mark shallow lines in the soil, called drills. They should be about 1 cm deep and 15 to 20 cm apart. Carrot rows may be a little wider.
2. Mix the tiny seeds with dry sand or fine dry soil. Mixed seeds spread more thinly and evenly when you sprinkle them.
3. Sprinkle the mixture thinly along the drill. Do not drop clumps of seeds in one place.
4. Cover the seeds with a thin layer of fine soil. Press very gently with the palm of your hand or the back of a rake. This helps seeds touch the soil and take in moisture.
5. Cover the bed with a thin layer of dry grass to keep the soil moist and protect seeds from heavy rain and birds. Remove it as soon as seedlings start to appear.
6. Water gently with a fine spray so seeds do not wash away.
7. Label the row with the name of the crop and the date sown.

### Remember
The rule of thumb is that the smaller the seed, the shallower it must be sown.

## PAGE 4: Caring for the seedlings
After sowing, the work is not over. Seeds and seedlings need care to become healthy plants. Carrot seeds may take one to three weeks to sprout, so be patient.

### Watering
Keep the soil moist but not soaking. Water every day, in the morning or evening, until the seedlings are up. Later water when the soil is dry.

### Thinning
When tiny seeds grow, too many seedlings come up close together. They fight for light, water and food. Thinning means removing extra seedlings so the strong ones have room.
• Wait until the seedlings are about 3 to 5 cm tall.
• Water the bed first so the soil is soft.
• Gently pull the weaker seedlings so those left are a few centimetres apart. For carrots, leave about 5 cm between plants.
• Firm the soil around those left.

### Weeding
Weeds take water and food from the crop. Pull them by hand when small, taking care not to pull the seedlings. Weeding is easiest after rain or watering.

### Protection
Keep off hens, goats and children from walking on the bed. A scarecrow or a simple cover can protect the seeds from birds.

### Look for problems
If the seedlings turn yellow or leaves have holes, ask a teacher or adult what may be wrong. It may be too little water, too many plants, or insects.

### When to harvest
• Radish: about 4 to 6 weeks after sowing.
• Carrots: about 3 to 4 months, when the tops are strong and the root has become thick and orange.
• Amaranth leaves: can be picked when the plants are about a hand high, and picking continues for weeks.
• Millet: the heads are ripe when they turn brown and the grains are hard. This takes about three to four months.

### Harvesting carrots
Water the ground first so that the soil is soft. Loosen the soil beside the carrot with a fork or trowel. Hold the leaves near the top and pull gently. Wash the carrots and eat or sell them.

### Why growing food matters
Growing tiny-seeded crops helps a family to have vegetables and grain at home. It saves money and gives fresh food. Extra produce can be sold in the local market.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
Direct sowing means sowing seeds where the crop will stay. Carrots, millet, amaranth and radish are grown this way. A fine seedbed, shallow sowing and good care give a healthy crop.

### Steps to remember
1. Prepare a fine, level seedbed.
2. Make shallow drills.
3. Mix seeds with sand and sprinkle thinly.
4. Cover lightly, mulch and water gently.
5. Thin, weed and protect.

### Common mistakes
• Leaving big lumps in the seedbed. Tiny seeds cannot push through them.
• Sowing too deep. Seeds run out of food before they reach the light.
• Sowing too thickly and forgetting to thin.
• Watering with a strong stream that washes seeds away.
• Letting the seedbed dry out before the seeds sprout.
• Forgetting to label the rows, so you do not know what is growing.

### Project idea
Sow a row of radish or amaranth in a box at school. Count the days until the seedlings appear. Record the number in your exercise book, then measure the height of one seedling each week.

## QUIZ
1. What is direct sowing?
   A) Sowing seeds in a nursery and moving them later
   B) Sowing seeds where the crop will grow until harvest
   C) Buying seedlings from a shop
   D) Watering the seedbed
   Answer: B
   Why: In direct sowing the seeds are sown in the final place and the seedlings are not moved.
2. Which crop is normally established by direct sowing of tiny seeds?
   A) Carrot
   B) Banana
   C) Mango tree
   D) Sweet potato vines
   Answer: A
   Why: Carrots are sown directly because moving young carrots damages the root.
3. Why should a seedbed for tiny seeds be fine and level?
   A) Tiny seeds cannot push through big lumps of soil
   B) It looks tidy
   C) It saves water for the whole year
   D) Lumps help seeds to grow deeper
   Answer: A
   Why: Tiny seeds have little stored food, so they need soft, fine soil to come up.
4. What is thinning?
   A) Adding more seeds
   B) Removing extra seedlings so the rest have room
   C) Making the soil thinner
   D) Making the water less
   Answer: B
   Why: Thinning removes extra seedlings so the strongest can grow with enough space.
5. Why do we mix tiny seeds with dry sand before sowing? (2 marks)
   Answer: So the seeds spread out thinly and evenly instead of falling in clumps.
   Why: 1 mark for spreading thinly/evenly, 1 mark for avoiding crowding or clumps.
6. Name three crops that are sown directly by tiny seeds. (3 marks)
   Answer: Carrots, millet, amaranth, radish or sorghum (any three).
   Why: 1 mark for each correct crop.
7. Describe how to prepare a fine seedbed. (4 marks)
   Answer: Clear weeds and stones; dig the soil to loosen it; break lumps until fine; mix in compost; level with a rake.
   Why: 1 mark for each of four correct steps.
8. Explain why tiny seeds should be sown at a shallow depth. (2 marks)
   Answer: They have little stored food, so if buried deeply they run out of energy before reaching the surface and light.
   Why: 1 mark for little food stored, 1 mark for not being able to reach the surface.
9. A learner sowed carrot seeds and many seedlings came up crowded together. What should the learner do, and why? (3 marks)
   Answer: Thin the seedlings by removing the weaker ones so that about 5 cm remain between plants, because crowded plants compete for water, light and food.
   Why: 1 mark for thinning, 1 mark for the spacing, 1 mark for the reason.
