## PAGE 1: What is an open-ended composition?
A composition is a piece of writing in which you put your own ideas in order. An open-ended composition gives you a topic or the beginning of a story, and you decide the rest. There is no single correct story. Your ideas, your characters and your ending make the story yours.

### Examples of open-ended topics
• A day I will never forget
• The best celebration I have attended
• The day the guests arrived late
• A story that begins: "The drums began to beat and everyone ran to the compound..."

We write to express our thoughts and feelings clearly. When we write clearly, the reader can share what we saw, felt and thought. Ask yourself: what do I want my reader to imagine? How can I make my story interesting?

### Ways to make a story interesting
• Start with something that makes the reader curious.
• Tell who, where and when.
• Add small details: colours, smells, sounds.
• Use direct words such as "the sweet smell of pilau" rather than "good food".
• Use one or two similes, such as "as bright as a new coin".
• End with a feeling or a lesson.

Before we write, we must plan. A story without a plan wanders like a lost goat.

## PAGE 2: Planning your composition
Good writers plan before they write. A plan is a short list of ideas in order. It does not need full sentences.

### Steps in planning
1. Choose the topic. Read it carefully.
2. Brainstorm. Write every idea that comes to mind. Talk to a friend.
3. Choose the best ideas and cross out those that do not fit.
4. Put them in order: beginning, middle and end.

### A sample plan for "A day I will never forget"
Beginning: My birthday, at home in Machakos, Saturday morning.
Middle: Grandparents arrive, cake is missing, we find it in the kitchen cupboard.
End: We share the cake, I feel happy and thankful.

## PAGE 3: Writing the paragraphs
A paragraph is a group of sentences about one idea. When you start a new idea, start a new line and leave a small gap at the start of the line.

### Parts of a composition
• Introduction (first paragraph): tell the reader who, where and when. Make the reader want to read on.
• Body (middle paragraphs): tell what happened in order. One idea in each paragraph.
• Conclusion (last paragraph): say how it ended, or how you felt.

### Useful linking words
first, then, next, after that, soon, suddenly, at last, finally.

### Punctuation and spelling
• Begin every sentence with a capital letter.
• End with a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark.
• Use capital letters for names: Mary, Kisumu, Saturday.
• Check the spelling of words from your word bank in this unit: celebrate, ceremony, invitation, guest.

### Write the first paragraph
Example: "It was a bright Saturday morning in Machakos. I woke up early because it was my tenth birthday. The sun was as warm as a fresh loaf of bread, and I could smell mandazi from the kitchen."

Notice that it tells when (Saturday morning), where (Machakos) and who (I). It also has one simile. Now write your own first paragraph on the topic you have chosen.

## PAGE 4: A model composition
Read this composition carefully. Then look at how it is built.

### The Birthday Cake That Disappeared
It was a bright Saturday morning in Machakos. I woke up early because it was my tenth birthday. The sun was as warm as a fresh loaf of bread, and I could smell mandazi from the kitchen.

By ten o'clock, my grandparents had arrived. Everyone was ready to sing, but when Mother went to bring the cake, it was gone. "Who has taken my cake?" she asked. We looked under the table and behind the door. My little brother Musa began to laugh.

At last we found the cake in the kitchen cupboard. Musa had hidden it as a surprise! We sang, ate and danced until the evening. It was the happiest day I can remember.

### Look at the parts
• Introduction: time, place and person. It sets the scene.
• Body: the problem (the cake is gone) and the search.
• Conclusion: the solution and the feeling.
• Similes, linking words (by ten o'clock, at last) and direct speech ("Who has taken my cake?") make it lively.

The composition has about 130 words. In class, your teacher will tell you how long yours should be.

## PAGE 5: Writing, checking and sharing
### Write the whole composition
1. Look at your plan.
2. Write the introduction, the body and the conclusion. Use your best handwriting.
3. Write on your own. Your ideas must be your own.

### Proofread
To proofread means to read your work carefully to find and fix mistakes. Then swap with a friend and check each other's work.

### Proofreading checklist
• Does every sentence begin with a capital letter and end with the right punctuation?
• Are the names written with capital letters?
• Are the words spelt correctly? Check the difficult ones in a dictionary.
• Does the story have a beginning, a middle and an end?
• Does each paragraph stay with one idea?
• Is the handwriting neat and easy to read?

Mark mistakes lightly with a pencil, and then correct them when you rewrite.

### Share your work
Read your story aloud to your group. Listen to others' stories and say one thing you liked. You can also read stories online or in a book, with a teacher's guidance, to see how other writers begin and end their stories.

### Remember
Plan first. Use paragraphs. Check your work. Rewrite neatly. Be proud of your own ideas.

## QUIZ
1. What is the first step in writing a good composition?
   A) Writing the conclusion
   B) Planning your ideas
   C) Copying a friend's story
   D) Drawing a picture
   Answer: B
   Why: A plan helps you put ideas in order before you start.
2. Which part of a composition tells who, where and when?
   A) The conclusion
   B) The body
   C) The introduction
   D) The title only
   Answer: C
   Why: The introduction sets the scene by telling who, where and when.
3. Which linking word shows the end of a story?
   A) first
   B) then
   C) next
   D) finally
   Answer: D
   Why: Finally is used to introduce the last event.
4. What does "proofread" mean?
   A) To write faster
   B) To read your work carefully and fix mistakes
   C) To draw pictures
   D) To read it to the teacher without changes
   Answer: B
   Why: Proofreading is checking spelling, punctuation and sense.
5. A new idea in a composition should begin
   A) in a new paragraph
   B) in the same sentence
   C) on a new page
   D) with a question mark
   Answer: A
   Why: A paragraph holds one idea. A new idea needs a new paragraph.
6. List the three main parts of a composition. (2 marks)
   Answer: Introduction, body and conclusion.
   Why: 1 mark for two parts correct and 2 marks for all three.
7. Write an introduction of three sentences for the topic "The day the guests arrived late". (3 marks)
   Answer: For example: It was Sunday afternoon in Kisumu. Our whole family was ready for the party, but the guests had not come. The cake was cold and the music had stopped.
   Why: 1 mark for telling when or where, 1 mark for who or what is happening, 1 mark for correct sentences with capital letters and full stops.
8. Give three questions you should ask when you proofread your work. (3 marks)
   Answer: Any three, for example: Does every sentence start with a capital letter? Are words spelt correctly? Does my story have a beginning, middle and end?
   Why: 1 mark for each sensible question.
9. Explain two ways to make a story interesting. (4 marks)
   Answer: Start in a way that makes the reader curious, and use small details or a simile so that the reader can picture the scene. Other correct ideas are dialogue and a good ending.
   Why: 2 marks for each way, with 1 mark for naming it and 1 mark for explaining it.
