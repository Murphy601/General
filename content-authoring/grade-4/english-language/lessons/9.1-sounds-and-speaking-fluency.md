## PAGE 1: Speaking fluently
Fluent speaking means talking clearly, at a good speed, without long stops or too many "um" and "er" sounds. A fluent speaker is easy to follow and sounds confident.

### How to speak fluently
1. Know your topic. Think about what you want to say.
2. Speak at a calm speed. Not too fast, or people miss words; not too slow, or people lose interest.
3. Pause between ideas, not in the middle of them.
4. Use a clear voice that everyone can hear.
5. Use your face and hands to help your meaning.
6. Look at your audience.

### Nonverbal cues
• A smile shows you are friendly.
• Eye contact shows you are honest and interested.
• A nod means yes or agreement.
• Open hands show that you are welcoming.
• Standing straight shows confidence.
• Hands over your mouth or looking at the floor may make it hard for people to hear you.

### The theme
This theme is about HIV and AIDS. When we speak about health, we must speak with care and respect. We use correct facts and we never laugh at, or blame, people who are sick.

### Try it
Stand and speak for one minute about your favourite meal. Try not to stop. A friend counts the number of times you stopped or said "um".

## PAGE 2: The sounds /s/ and /z/
The letter s can say two different sounds.

### /s/ (a hiss, no voice)
Put your fingers on your throat. You should feel no buzzing.
• sun, sit, sick, see, sad
• miss, kiss, class, pass
• cats, books, hats (the plural s says /s/ here)

### /z/ (a buzz, with voice)
Put your fingers on your throat. You feel buzzing.
• zoo, zebra, zip, zero
• easy, busy, music, please
• dogs, beds, hens, bags (the s says /z/ after voiced sounds)

### Endings
The plural -s says:
• /s/ after sounds like p, t, k, f: cups, cats, books, chiefs
• /z/ after most other sounds: dogs, pens, girls, boys

### Sentences to practise
1. "Six sick children sat in the sun."
2. "The zebra at the zoo was busy eating."
3. "Please pass the soup to Susan."
4. "Dogs and cats live in houses." (dogs has /z/, cats has /s/)

### Common mistake
Do not change /z/ to /s/. "Zebra" is not "sebra"; "please" is not "pleas".

## PAGE 3: The sounds /h/ and /f/
### /h/
Breathe out gently through an open mouth, as if you were fogging a mirror. There is no voice, and your tongue does not touch the roof of your mouth.
• hand, house, hospital, health
• hen, help, hot, home
• behind, ahead, perhaps

Be careful: some words start with the letter h but the h is silent: hour, honest, honour. You say "our" and "onest".

### /f/
Bite your lower lip lightly with your upper teeth and blow air. There is no voice.
• fish, food, five, family
• fever, fun, fresh, foot
• life, leaf, half, safe

Note: /f/ can also be spelled "ph" (phone, photograph) or "gh" (laugh, cough).

### Compare /h/ and /f/
• hair, fair
• hill, fill
• hat, fat
• heel, feel

### Sentences to practise
1. "Half of the family had fresh fish for food."
2. "Help the hen behind the house."
3. "Have fun, but stay safe."
4. "The health worker helped my friend feel fine."

## PAGE 4: The digraph "gh"
A digraph is two letters that make one sound. The letters "gh" are tricky because they can say /f/ or be silent.

### "gh" says /f/
• laugh
• cough
• enough
• rough
• tough
• laughter

### "gh" is silent
• night, light, right, fight, sight
• high, sigh, thigh
• daughter, neighbour, though, through, thought

### At the start of a word
"gh" at the start says /g/: ghost, ghastly.

### How to tell
You cannot always tell from the spelling, so learn them as word families. Words ending in "ough" can say /f/ (enough, rough, tough, cough) or other sounds (though, through, thought). Look them up in a dictionary if you are not sure.

### Sentences to practise
1. "It was a rough night, and the child had a cough."
2. "Is that enough light for the meeting?"
3. "My daughter laughed at the tough sums."
4. "Through the night, the nurse thought about her patient."

### Health words with these sounds
health, hospital, safe, sick, fever, cough, blood, medicine, cure, infect.

## PAGE 5: Vocabulary for the theme
Learn these words and use them politely and accurately.
• virus: a tiny germ that can make people ill
• infect: to pass a germ to someone
• affect: to make a change in someone or something
• spread: to move from one person or place to another
• signs: things that show that something is happening (for example, a fever)
• prevention: acting so that something bad does not happen
• cure: a medicine that makes an illness go away completely
• medicine: something taken to treat or prevent illness
• sharp object: a thing with a point or edge that can cut, such as a razor blade or needle
• diet: the food we eat regularly
• tuberculosis (TB): an illness of the lungs that causes a long cough

### Sentences
1. "Washing hands helps in the prevention of many illnesses."
2. "A good diet helps the body stay strong."
3. "We must not share sharp objects like razor blades."
4. "A virus is too small to see without a special microscope."

### Speaking respectfully about illness
People who are ill need care, not fear. Use words like "person living with" instead of harsh words. You cannot get HIV by playing, hugging, sharing a cup or sitting together.

### Fill in the gaps
Choose from: virus, prevention, medicine.
1. Taking ____ on time helps people get better. (medicine)
2. ____ is better than cure. (Prevention)
3. A ____ can make a person ill. (virus)

## PAGE 6: Speaking practice and a short talk
### Reciting a poem at the right pace
Read this poem in a clear voice. Take a breath at the end of each line.

Wash your hands, keep them clean,
Eat good food that's fresh and green.
Care for those who are not well,
Kind words help their hearts to swell.

### Plan a one-minute talk
Choose one topic:
• "How we can care for a sick friend"
• "Why we must not share sharp objects"
• "Healthy food for a strong body"
Use this frame:
1. Start: "Good morning, everyone. Today I will talk about..."
2. Middle: two or three points with a reason for each.
3. End: "Thank you for listening."

### Checklist for your talk
• Did I speak clearly and slowly enough?
• Did I look at my audience?
• Did I use nonverbal cues?
• Did I pronounce /s/, /z/, /h/, /f/ and gh words correctly?
• Did I speak without long pauses?

### Summary
• /s/ has no voice; /z/ has a buzz.
• /h/ is a soft breath; /f/ uses teeth and lip.
• "gh" may say /f/ (laugh) or be silent (night).
• Fluent speaking is clear, calm and confident.

## QUIZ
1. Which word ends with the sound /f/?
   A) night
   B) cough
   C) high
   D) daughter
   Answer: B
   Why: In "cough" the letters "gh" say /f/. In the other words "gh" is silent.
2. In which word does the letter s say /z/?
   A) sun
   B) sit
   C) easy
   D) class
   Answer: C
   Why: The s in "easy" is buzzed. In the other words s says /s/.
3. Which word has a silent h?
   A) hand
   B) hospital
   C) honest
   D) help
   Answer: C
   Why: "Honest" is said "onest".
4. Which is a sign of good speaking fluency?
   A) Stopping after every word
   B) Speaking so quietly that nobody hears
   C) Speaking clearly at a steady pace
   D) Looking at the floor
   Answer: C
   Why: Fluent speakers are clear, steady and confident.
5. Write two words in which "gh" is silent and two in which it says /f/. (4 marks)
   Answer: Silent: night, light, high, daughter (any two). /f/: laugh, cough, enough, rough (any two).
   Why: One mark for each correct word.
6. Give two nonverbal cues that show a speaker is confident. (2 marks)
   Answer: For example: eye contact and standing straight; a smile.
   Why: One mark for each.
7. Explain the meaning of "prevention" and give a sentence with the word. (3 marks)
   Answer: Prevention means acting so that something bad does not happen. For example: "Washing hands is one way of prevention."
   Why: Two marks for the meaning, one mark for a correct sentence.
8. Write a short talk of about four sentences on "How we can care for a sick friend." (4 marks)
   Answer: Any four sentences with a greeting, two or three caring actions, and a closing.
   Why: One mark each for the opening, two points and the closing.
