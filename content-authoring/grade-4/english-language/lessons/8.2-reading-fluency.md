## PAGE 1: What is fluent reading?
A fluent reader reads smoothly, like a person talking. The words come easily, the voice rises and falls, and the listener understands the message. A reader who is not yet fluent may stop at every word, guess wrongly or read in a flat voice.

Fluency has three parts:
• Accuracy: reading the words correctly, without leaving out or changing words.
• Speed (pace): reading not too slowly and not too fast. About the pace of a calm conversation is right.
• Expression: using your voice to show meaning, such as pausing at full stops and commas, and raising your voice for questions.

### Why fluency matters
If you spend all your effort decoding words, you have little left to think about the meaning. When reading is smooth, you can concentrate on the story or the facts. That helps you understand and remember what you read. Fluent reading is also a joy to listen to when you read aloud to a class.

### The wrong kind of fast
Fast reading is not the same as good reading. If you rush and make mistakes, you have to go back. Read at a speed where you get the words right and the meaning clear.

### Try it
Read the sentence below three times. First, in a flat voice. Second, very fast. Third, at a natural pace with expression. Which is best?
"The farmer looked at the sky, and then he smiled."

## PAGE 2: Strategies for fluent reading
Use these strategies to improve.

### 1. Preview
Look at the title, pictures and headings. Ask, "What is this text about?"

### 2. Read in phrases, not word by word
Instead of "The - farmer - went - to - the - field", read "The farmer / went to the field". Your eyes can group words into chunks that make sense.

### 3. Follow the punctuation
• Pause briefly at a comma.
• Pause a little longer at a full stop.
• Raise your voice at the end of a question.
• Use a stronger voice for an exclamation mark.

### 4. Re-read
Read the same passage two or three times. Each time your speed and expression will improve. Many good readers do this.

### 5. Read along with a model
Listen to your teacher or a recording, then read with them, then alone.

### 6. Use context for hard words
Look at the words around the hard word. If you cannot work it out, break it into syllables: har-vest, fer-ti-liz-er.

### Practise a tongue-friendly line
"Slowly the tractor turned, and the fresh brown soil rolled over."
Read it in chunks: Slowly the tractor turned / and the fresh brown soil / rolled over.

## PAGE 3: A passage for fluent reading (part 1)
Read this passage aloud to a partner. Read it twice. Listen for the pauses.

A Day at Mama Neema's Farm

Every school holiday, Kiprono and his sister Cherop go to their grandmother's farm near Kitale. Everybody calls her Mama Neema. She is a busy woman who wakes up before the sun.

On the first morning, the children heard the cockerel crow. "Wake up, farmers!" Mama Neema called. "The maize is waiting for us."

The maize field was green and tall. Between the rows, weeds were growing. Mama Neema gave each child a small hoe. "Weeds are thieves," she said. "They steal water and food from the maize. We must remove them."

Cherop worked quickly, but she pulled up a young maize plant by mistake. "Oh no!" she cried.

"Don't worry," Mama Neema said gently. "Everyone makes mistakes. Look carefully at the leaves. Maize leaves are long and smooth. Weeds are usually short and rough. Now you know."

Kiprono learnt fast. By midday, the children had cleared two long rows. Their arms were tired, but they were proud.

## PAGE 4: A passage for fluent reading (part 2) and questions
Continue reading. This part has about the same length again. In total, the passage is near 300 words.

At noon, they sat under a mango tree. Mama Neema brought out boiled maize, sweet potatoes and a jug of cold water. "Farming is hard work," she said, "but it feeds everyone. The food in the market, the porridge you eat for breakfast, all begin in a field like this."

After lunch, Mama Neema showed the children her compost heap. "This is where we throw vegetable peelings and dry leaves," she explained. "After a few months, they turn into rich soil. We do not need to buy much fertilizer. The compost feeds the plants."

"Does it smell?" asked Cherop, holding her nose.

"A little," said Mama Neema, laughing. "But it is a good smell, because it means the soil is healthy."

In the evening, the children helped to bring the goats into the shed. Mama Neema counted them. "Twelve, thirteen, fourteen. Every one is home." She closed the door and smiled. "Tomorrow we will plant beans."

That night, Kiprono and Cherop fell asleep quickly. They dreamed of green fields and cold water under a mango tree.

### Answer the questions
1. Where does Mama Neema live? (near Kitale)
2. Why does she call weeds thieves? (They steal water and food from the maize.)
3. How can you tell a maize plant from a weed in the story? (Maize leaves are long and smooth; weeds are usually short and rough.)
4. What is compost made from? (Vegetable peelings and dry leaves.)
5. Why does the compost heap smell? What does the smell show? (It shows the soil is healthy.)

## PAGE 5: Reading with expression
Expression makes a reader interesting. Here is how to add it.

### Voice
• Speak louder for excitement: "Oh no!" she cried.
• Speak softly for gentle words: "Don't worry," Mama Neema said gently.
• Change your voice for different speakers. Mama Neema's voice is calm; Cherop's may be quick and worried.

### Pace
• Slow down for sad or serious parts.
• Speed up a little for exciting parts.
• Pause before an important word.

### Reader's theatre
Reader's theatre needs no costumes or stage. Each person reads a part from a script, using only the voice. Try this script from the passage.

Narrator: The children were working in the maize field.
Cherop: Oh no! I pulled up a maize plant!
Mama Neema: Don't worry. Everyone makes mistakes. Look carefully at the leaves.
Kiprono: Maize leaves are long and smooth.
Mama Neema: That is right. Now you know.

Three or four learners read it, and they change parts after each go.

### Poems and songs
Rhythm is the beat in a poem or song. To read a poem well, notice where the beats fall and let your voice follow them.
"Pick up the hoe and dig, dig, dig, / Sow the seed, both small and big."
Tap your finger on the desk as you read to feel the beat.

## PAGE 6: Checking your reading
You can check your speed. Ask a partner to time you as you read a passage of 100 words. Try to read it in about one minute at a natural pace, with few mistakes. This is a guide; comfort and understanding matter more than a stopwatch.

### Fluency checklist
• Did I read words correctly?
• Did I read at a steady pace?
• Did I pause at full stops and commas?
• Did I change my voice to show feeling?
• Could my partner understand?

### Digital reading
When you read on a tablet or computer, keep safe. Use only texts your teacher or parent has chosen. Sit well, and rest your eyes often. You can also listen to a recording and read along.

### Fluency and understanding
After you read, answer inference questions. An inference is something the text does not say directly, but you can work out.
Example: "Kiprono learnt fast." Is he a good listener? Probably yes, because he learnt after only one lesson.

### Summary
• Fluency is accuracy, speed and expression.
• Read in phrases and follow punctuation.
• Re-read to get better.
• Reader's theatre and poems build fluency.
• Always check that you understand what you read.

## QUIZ
1. Which is NOT part of fluency?
   A) Accuracy
   B) Speed
   C) Expression
   D) Copying spelling
   Answer: D
   Why: Fluency means accuracy, suitable speed and expression.
2. How should your voice change when you read a question?
   A) It should rise at the end
   B) It should become silent
   C) It should be very loud
   D) It should not change
   Answer: A
   Why: A question ends with a rising voice, marked by the question mark.
3. Why did Mama Neema call weeds "thieves"?
   A) They eat the goats
   B) They steal water and food from the maize
   C) They take the hoes
   D) They make the field smell
   Answer: B
   Why: The passage says weeds steal water and food from the maize.
4. What is compost made from, according to the passage?
   A) Plastic bags
   B) Vegetable peelings and dry leaves
   C) Stones and sand
   D) Maize flour
   Answer: B
   Why: Mama Neema said the compost heap holds vegetable peelings and dry leaves.
5. Give two ways to become a more fluent reader. (2 marks)
   Answer: For example: re-read the passage; read in phrases; follow punctuation; read along with a recording.
   Why: One mark for each correct strategy.
6. Explain why fast reading is not always good reading. (2 marks)
   Answer: If you read too fast you make mistakes and miss meaning, so you have to go back.
   Why: One mark for mistakes, one mark for losing understanding.
7. From the passage, what can you tell about Mama Neema's character? Give one reason. (3 marks)
   Answer: She is kind and patient. When Cherop pulled up the maize by mistake, she did not scold but gently showed her how to tell the difference.
   Why: One mark for a fair trait, two marks for a reason from the text.
8. Read this sentence aloud and describe how your voice should change: "Oh no!" she cried. (3 marks)
   Answer: The voice should get louder and show worry or surprise at "Oh no!", then the reader should use a plain voice for "she cried".
   Why: One mark for louder voice, one for showing feeling, one for changing the voice for the narrator's words.
