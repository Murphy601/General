## PAGE 1: Stories from pictures
A pictorial composition is a story that you write by looking at a set of pictures. The pictures show what happens, and you use words to tell it. It is like being a film-maker and a writer at the same time.

Pictures can make us feel many things: happy, curious, worried or surprised. How do pictures make you feel? When you write a pictorial composition, you can share those feelings with your reader.

### What is different about a pictorial composition?
• The pictures give you the events, the people and the place. You do not have to invent everything.
• Your job is to look closely and tell the story in the right order, in your own words.
• You add details that are not in the pictures, such as what people say, think and feel.

### Where can you find pictures?
• Comics and picture books.
• Newspapers and magazines.
• Posters and photographs.
• Videos and websites, used with the help of a teacher or parent.
When you choose a picture online or from a book, choose pictures that tell a story and are suitable for children.

### Start by looking
Look at a picture in your book. Who is there? Where are they? What are they doing? What might happen next? Say your ideas aloud to a friend.

## PAGE 2: Reading a picture series
Follow these steps to prepare.

### Steps
1. Look at all the pictures first. Do not start writing yet.
2. Number the pictures in the correct order if they are jumbled. Look for clues: time, movement and expressions.
3. For each picture, ask: Who? Where? What is happening? How do they feel?
4. Make notes: two or three words for each picture.
5. Think of a title.

### Notes for the four pictures
1. Baraka, computer, types to Grandmother.
2. Clicks Send, excited.
3. Message goes through the internet.
4. Grandmother reads, smiles.

## PAGE 3: Turning pictures into sentences
Now we turn notes into sentences. Use the past tense, as though the story has already happened: typed, clicked, travelled, read, smiled.

### Tips
• Write at least one or two sentences for each picture.
• Use linking words to move from one picture to the next: first, then, soon, after that, at last, in the end.
• Add feelings and small details: "He was excited." "Her face lit up."
• Add words that people say, if it helps: "Grandmother, I miss you," he wrote.
• Keep the same tense all the way through the story.

### Sentences for picture 1
• One afternoon, Baraka sat at the computer in his uncle's house.
• He typed a message to his grandmother.

### Sentences for picture 2
• When he finished, he read it again.
• Then he clicked the Send button.

### Sentences for picture 3
• The message travelled across the internet in a few seconds.

### Sentences for picture 4
• At last, Grandmother read the message.
• She smiled because she was happy to hear from him.

### Beginning, middle and end
A pictorial composition also has a beginning (picture 1), middle (pictures 2 and 3) and end (picture 4). Give it a title that fits the story, such as "A Message for Grandmother".

## PAGE 4: A model pictorial composition
Here is a composition of about 65 words based on the four pictures. It follows the story in order.

### A Message for Grandmother
One afternoon, Baraka sat at the computer in his uncle's house. He typed a message to his grandmother. When he finished, he read it again and clicked the Send button. The message travelled across the internet in a few seconds. At last, Grandmother read it and smiled. She was very happy to hear from him.

### Look at it closely
• It has a title.
• The events are in the same order as the pictures.
• Time words: one afternoon, when, at last.
• The past tense is used all through: sat, typed, read, travelled, smiled.
• It has a beginning, middle and end.
• It is between 60 and 80 words.

### Picture series for practice
Here is another series, described in words. Write your own composition.
Picture 1: A girl sits in front of a school computer and sees a message that says "You have won a prize! Click here."
Picture 2: The girl looks worried and does not click.
Picture 3: She calls her teacher and points to the screen.
Picture 4: The teacher explains that it is a trick, and the girl thanks her and smiles.

Plan first: name the girl, give the place and time, write one sentence per picture and add a feeling. Then write 60 to 80 words.

## PAGE 5: Write, check and share
### Write your composition
1. Look at your pictures again.
2. Write a draft of 60 to 80 words.
3. Read it aloud to see whether it makes sense.
4. Write a clean copy in neat handwriting.
5. Give it a title.

### Working with a partner
You can discuss the pictures with a partner, take turns to describe each picture and write your own sentences. When you are finished, compare stories. They will be different, and that is good, because each writer has a different voice.

### Checklist
• Does my story follow the order of the pictures?
• Did I use the past tense all the way through?
• Did I use linking words?
• Did I say how people felt?
• Did I begin every sentence with a capital letter and end with a full stop?
• Did I use a title?
• Is it 60 to 80 words?

### Create your own pictures
Draw four pictures of your own, for example a morning routine, a visit to the market or a lesson on internet safety. Swap with a friend, and write the story of each other's pictures.

### Watch and write
With permission, watch a short video about the theme. Note the main events. Then write a pictorial composition about it.

### Remember
Look, order, plan, write, check. Pictures give you the ideas. Your words make the story come alive.

## QUIZ
1. What is a pictorial composition?
   A) A story written from a set of pictures
   B) A list of numbers
   C) A song
   D) A map
   Answer: A
   Why: A pictorial composition uses pictures to guide the events of the story.
2. What should you do first when you get a series of pictures?
   A) Start writing at once
   B) Look at all the pictures and put them in order
   C) Copy a friend's work
   D) Write the ending only
   Answer: B
   Why: Looking at all the pictures helps you understand the story before writing.
3. Which tense is best for telling the story?
   A) Past tense
   B) Future tense only
   C) A different tense in each sentence
   D) No verbs at all
   Answer: A
   Why: Stories are usually told in the past tense, and we keep the same tense all through.
4. Which words help us move from one picture to the next?
   A) first, then, at last
   B) big, small, tall
   C) mango, milk, rice
   D) red, green, blue
   Answer: A
   Why: They are linking words that show order.
5. About how many words should a Grade 4 pictorial composition have?
   A) 5 to 10
   B) 20 to 30
   C) 60 to 80
   D) 300 to 400
   Answer: C
   Why: The length should be about 60 to 80 words.
6. Write a title and one opening sentence for a story about a girl who finds a lost mobile phone. (2 marks)
   Answer: For example: Title: An Honest Girl. Opening: One morning, Achieng found a phone near the school gate.
   Why: 1 mark for a suitable title and 1 mark for a correct opening sentence.
7. List three steps you follow before you write a pictorial composition. (3 marks)
   Answer: Look at all the pictures, put them in order, ask who, where and what, make notes, plan a title.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct step, up to 3.
8. Rewrite in the past tense: "Baraka types a message and clicks Send." (2 marks)
   Answer: Baraka typed a message and clicked Send.
   Why: 1 mark for typed and 1 mark for clicked.
9. Write about 60 words about these three pictures: (1) A boy plants a seed. (2) He waters it every day. (3) A tall plant grows. Use linking words. (5 marks)
   Answer: A composition of about 60 words such as: One morning, Musa planted a seed in the garden. Every day he watered it. After some weeks, a small green shoot appeared. At last, a tall plant grew and Musa was proud.
   Why: 1 mark for a beginning, 1 mark for events in order, 1 mark for linking words, 1 mark for the past tense and 1 mark for punctuation and length.
