## PAGE 1: Why read on your own?
Intensive reading is when the whole class reads one short text and studies it closely. Extensive reading is different. You choose a book, a story or a poem and read it by yourself, for pleasure and to learn. You read a lot, and you read often.

People who read independently gain many things:
• They learn new words without a teacher explaining each one.
• They spell better, because they see words again and again.
• They write better, because they have seen how good writers put sentences together.
• They learn about places, people and ideas far from their own home.
• They enjoy quiet time and a good story.

### Kinds of materials
• Narratives: stories that tell what happened to someone, such as folktales and adventure stories.
• Poems: short pieces in lines, often with rhyme and rhythm.
• Graded readers: books written at different levels of difficulty, so that you can find one that suits your reading.
• Print and electronic texts: printed books, magazines and screen texts.

### Think about it
What kind of material do you enjoy most? Why? Tell a partner. Someone else may enjoy a different kind, and that is fine.

## PAGE 2: Choosing a book that suits you
Choose a book that is neither too easy nor too hard. Here is the five-finger test.
1. Open the book at any page and start reading.
2. Every time you meet a word you do not know, hold up one finger.
3. If you have used up all five fingers on one page, the book is probably too hard for now.
4. If you have used no fingers, the book may be too easy. That is fine for fun, but try something harder too.
5. If you have used one to three fingers, it is a good fit.

### Look at the book before you read
• Read the title and look at the cover picture. What might the book be about?
• Read the back cover. It gives a short summary.
• Read the first paragraph. Do you want to continue?

### Be honest and careful
Choose material that is right for your age. If a book or website makes you feel uncomfortable or afraid, close it and talk to a trusted adult. When you read on a screen, stay on pages that a teacher, parent or guardian has approved.

### Try it
Visit your school library or reading corner. Choose two books, one narrative and one collection of poems. Use the five-finger test on both. Write the titles and say which one you chose and why.

## PAGE 3: A narrative to read and think about
Read this short story slowly. Stop after each paragraph to picture what is happening.

The Boy and the Borrowed Phone

Otieno's aunt let him use her phone to look up a football score. While he was searching, a message appeared: "You have won a prize! Send your name and school to claim it."

Otieno felt excited. He typed his name and was about to type the name of his school. Then he remembered his teacher's words: "Never give personal details to someone you do not know."

He stopped. He showed the message to his aunt. She read it and laughed gently. "This is a trick, Otieno. Some people send messages like this to find out about children. You did well to ask me first."

Otieno deleted the message. His aunt showed him how to block the sender. Then she said, "Next time, you will know what to do."

Later, Otieno told his class about the message. Many learners had seen similar messages. They decided to make a poster: "Think before you tap. Ask a trusted adult."

### Retell it
To retell a story, say who, where, what happened and how it ended. For example: "Otieno was using his aunt's phone when a strange message said he had won a prize. He remembered what his teacher taught and showed the message to his aunt. She blocked the sender, and the class made a poster."

## PAGE 4: Reading a poem
Poems say much in few words. Some have rhyme, where lines end with the same sound. Most have rhythm, a beat that you can tap out.

Read this poem quietly, then aloud.

Books

Open a book and a door swings wide,
There are lions and rivers and boats to ride.
There are mountains to climb and stars to name,
And every page is a brand-new game.

When the rain comes down and the sun has gone,
A book in my hand keeps the fun going on.
So carry a book in your bag today,
And read a little along the way.

### Talk about the poem
• Which words rhyme? (wide and ride, name and game, gone and on, today and way)
• What does the poet compare a book to? A door.
• What does the poet say a book can give you? Adventures, information and fun when the weather is bad.

### Share your opinion
Say what you liked about the poem. Give a reason: "I liked the line about the door, because it made me imagine reading as going somewhere." Listen to your friends' opinions with respect. You do not have to agree.

## PAGE 5: Proverbs in the books you read
A proverb is a short, wise saying that people have repeated for a long time. Many stories and graded readers use proverbs. A proverb does not usually mean exactly what its words say. It teaches a lesson.

### Some well-known proverbs
• Slow and steady wins the race: If we work patiently and do not rush, we succeed.
• Look before you leap: Think carefully before you act.
• Many hands make light work: A job is easier when many people help.
• A friend in need is a friend indeed: A true friend helps you when you have trouble.

### How to work out a proverb
1. Read it in the story.
2. Ask what is happening in the story.
3. Ask what lesson the character learns.
4. Say the meaning in simpler words.

For example, Otieno stopped and thought before typing his school name. "Look before you leap" fits his story because he thought before acting.

### Try it
Pick a proverb from a book you are reading, or from those above. Tell the class what it means and give an example from real life.

## PAGE 6: Remembering, sharing and starting a reading club
You read more when you share what you have read. Here are ways to remember and share.

### Remember
• Write down three new words and their meanings in a small notebook.
• After each chapter, say one sentence about what happened.
• Draw a picture or a short story map with beginning, middle and end.

### Share
• Retell your story to a partner in your own words.
• Tell a group whether you would recommend the book, and why.
• Read your favourite lines aloud.

### Set up a reading club
1. Agree on a time each week, for example after lessons on Friday.
2. Choose a quiet place, such as the library or under a tree.
3. Each member reads a book they have chosen.
4. Take turns to talk about the book for one or two minutes.
5. Listen without interrupting.
6. Keep a list of the books read.
7. Keep to online safety rules if you read on a screen.

### Summary
• Independent reading builds vocabulary and confidence.
• Use the five-finger test to choose a book.
• Retell stories by saying who, where, what happened and how it ended.
• Proverbs teach lessons in few words.

## QUIZ
1. What is extensive reading?
   A) Reading one short text closely with the class
   B) Reading only for a test
   C) Reading many texts of your own choice for pleasure and learning
   D) Copying a story into your book
   Answer: C
   Why: Extensive reading means choosing materials and reading a lot on your own.
2. In the five-finger test, what does it mean if you use all five fingers on one page?
   A) The book is probably too hard for now
   B) The book is perfect
   C) The book is too short
   D) The book has too many pictures
   Answer: A
   Why: Five unknown words on a single page means many difficulties.
3. Which of these is a graded reader?
   A) A newspaper advert
   B) A book written at a level that suits a certain group of readers
   C) A test paper
   D) A list of names
   Answer: B
   Why: Graded readers are written at different levels of difficulty.
4. What does the proverb "Many hands make light work" mean?
   A) People with big hands are strong
   B) A job is easier when many people help
   C) Work should be done alone
   D) Hands should always be clean
   Answer: B
   Why: When people share a task, each has less to do.
5. In the story, why did Otieno stop typing the name of his school? (2 marks)
   Answer: He remembered his teacher's warning never to give personal details to someone he did not know.
   Why: One mark for the warning, one mark for linking it to the stranger's message.
6. Write the two pairs of rhyming words from the first verse of the poem "Books". (2 marks)
   Answer: wide and ride; name and game.
   Why: One mark for each correct pair. The pairs come at the ends of lines one and two, and lines three and four.
7. Name three things you can do to remember what you have read. (3 marks)
   Answer: For example: write new words and meanings; say one sentence about each chapter; draw a story map; retell the story to a partner.
   Why: One mark for each correct suggestion.
8. Retell the story of "The Boy and the Borrowed Phone" in three or four sentences. (4 marks)
   Answer: Otieno used his aunt's phone. A message said he had won a prize and asked for his name and school. He remembered his teacher's warning and showed the message to his aunt. She blocked the sender and the class made a poster.
   Why: One mark for the character and setting, one for the message, one for what he did, one for the ending.
