## PAGE 1: What is a narrative?
A narrative is a story. It tells what happened to someone, in the order in which it happened. A narrative may be a true event, such as "A Day on the Farm", or an imagined one, such as "The Talking Scarecrow".

Every narrative answers these questions:
• Who is in the story? (characters)
• Where does it happen? (setting)
• When does it happen?
• What happens? (events)
• How does it end?

### Why we write narratives
We write narratives to share experiences, to entertain, and to express our feelings. Our best stories often come from things we have seen, such as a visit to a farm, a market day or a football match.

### Tenses
A narrative is usually written in the past tense: "We walked", "The cow ran", "I felt afraid". Keep the same tense from the beginning to the end.

### Try it
Tell a partner one true story from your life in three sentences: how it began, what happened, how it ended. Your partner listens and asks one question about the story.

### Beginning (introduction)
Tell the reader who the story is about, where they are and when it happens. Make the first sentence interesting.
Example: "On Saturday morning, my cousin and I went to my uncle's farm in Nyeri."

### Middle (the events)
Tell what happened, one thing after another. Often there is a problem or an exciting moment.
Example: "We fed the chickens and picked tomatoes. Suddenly, a goat escaped from the shed."

### End (conclusion)
Tell how the story finishes. You can say how the problem was solved and how the characters felt.
Example: "We caught the goat together. I was tired but proud."

### Time words help the order
first, then, next, after that, suddenly, soon, at last, finally, in the end.

## PAGE 3: Organising ideas into a paragraph
A paragraph is a group of sentences about one idea. The first line starts a little to the right (indented) or there is a blank line before it.

### Ordering jumbled sentences
Here are five sentences in the wrong order. Put them in order.
(a) Finally, we carried the basket of tomatoes home.
(b) On Saturday, we went to my uncle's farm.
(c) Next, we picked ripe tomatoes.
(d) First, we fed the chickens.
(e) Uncle thanked us for our help.

Correct order: (b), (d), (c), (a), (e).
Paragraph: "On Saturday, we went to my uncle's farm. First, we fed the chickens. Next, we picked ripe tomatoes. Finally, we carried the basket of tomatoes home. Uncle thanked us for our help."

### Tips for ordering
• Look for the sentence that introduces the story; it usually mentions who, where or when.
• Look for time words: first, next, then, finally.
• The last sentence often gives the result or a feeling.

### Planning
Before writing, make a short plan:
Beginning: who, where, when
Middle: three events, one after the other
End: how it ended, and how I felt
A plan takes two minutes and saves you from forgetting the ending.

## PAGE 4: Making the story interesting with similes
A simile compares two different things using like or as. It helps the reader picture what you describe.

### Examples
• The tractor roared like a lion.
• The maize was as tall as a door.
• The goat ran like the wind.
• The soil was as dry as bone.
• My arms felt as heavy as stones.
• The cabbage leaves were as green as the grass after rain.

### How to make your own
1. Choose the thing you want to describe: the sun, a tractor, a tired farmer.
2. Think of its main quality: hot, loud, slow, tired.
3. Compare it to something that is very hot, loud, slow or tired.
Example: The sun was as hot as a burning coal.

### Other ways to make your story interesting
• Use exact verbs: "dashed" is more exciting than "went".
• Use describing words: "the muddy path", "the ripe, red tomatoes".
• Include what people say: "Look out!" shouted Uncle.
• Add feelings: I felt nervous, excited, proud.
• Use your five senses: what did you see, hear, smell, touch and taste?

### Do not overdo similes
One or two good similes are enough in a short story. Too many make the writing heavy.

## PAGE 5: A model narrative
Here is a model narrative of about 70 words. The school asked for 60 to 80 words.

A Visit to the Farm

Last Saturday, my class visited a farm near our school. We arrived early, when the grass was still wet. The farmer showed us cows, goats and a field of green cabbages. Suddenly, a calf escaped from the shed and ran like the wind. Everyone laughed as the farmer caught it. Before we left, we ate sweet mangoes. I was tired but very happy, and I learnt that farmers work hard.

### Study the model
• Beginning: "Last Saturday, my class visited a farm near our school."
• Middle: the farm animals, the calf that escaped.
• End: the mangoes and the writer's feelings.
• Simile: "ran like the wind".
• Past tense throughout: visited, arrived, showed, escaped, laughed.
• Time words: last Saturday, early, suddenly, before we left.

### Safety on a school visit
When you visit a farm, listen to the farmer and your teacher, stay with the group, wash your hands afterwards, and do not touch animals or machines unless you are told it is safe. These safety points are worth including in your story.

### Word count
Count your words. Write a first draft, count, and then add or remove sentences to fit 60 to 80 words.

## PAGE 6: Write, check and share
### Your task
Write a narrative of 60 to 80 words called "A Day on the Farm" or "My Visit to the Farm". You may write about a real visit, or imagine one.

### Steps
1. Choose a title.
2. Plan the beginning, middle and end.
3. Write the first draft in the past tense.
4. Add one simile and at least two time words.
5. Read your story aloud to yourself.
6. Check your work with the checklist below.

### Checklist
• Does it tell who, where and when?
• Do the events follow each other in order?
• Does it have an ending?
• Are all verbs in the past tense?
• Does each sentence begin with a capital letter and end with a full stop?
• Are names spelt with capital letters?
• Is it 60 to 80 words?

### Share
Read your story to a partner or the class. Say one kind thing about each story you hear, and one suggestion. You may write a neat copy and display it. Some learners type theirs on a computer with help from an adult.

### Summary
• A narrative tells a story in order.
• It has a beginning, a middle and an end.
• Use time words and past tense.
• Similes use like or as to compare.
• Plan, write, check and share.

## QUIZ
1. Which part of a narrative tells who, where and when?
   A) The beginning
   B) The middle
   C) The end
   D) The title only
   Answer: A
   Why: The beginning introduces the characters, setting and time.
2. Which sentence contains a simile?
   A) The goat was in the shed.
   B) The goat ran like the wind.
   C) The goat ate grass.
   D) The goat was brown.
   Answer: B
   Why: It compares the goat's running to the wind using "like".
3. In which tense is a narrative usually written?
   A) Future
   B) Past
   C) Present progressive
   D) Command form
   Answer: B
   Why: A narrative tells what happened, so it uses the past tense.
4. Which is a good time word to show the order of events?
   A) Green
   B) Suddenly
   C) Farm
   D) Heavy
   Answer: B
   Why: "Suddenly" shows when something happened. The others are not time words.
5. Arrange the sentences into a paragraph: (a) Finally, we went home. (b) We arrived at the farm at nine o'clock. (c) Then we fed the goats. (3 marks)
   Answer: We arrived at the farm at nine o'clock. Then we fed the goats. Finally, we went home.
   Why: One mark for each correct position: (b), (c), (a).
6. Write one simile using "as ... as" about the sun. (2 marks)
   Answer: For example: The sun was as hot as fire.
   Why: One mark for the "as ... as" pattern, one mark for a suitable comparison.
7. Describe the three parts of a narrative. (3 marks)
   Answer: Beginning: introduces who, where and when. Middle: events and a problem. End: how it is solved and how people felt.
   Why: One mark for each part described correctly.
8. Write a narrative of about 60 words about a visit to a farm. Include a beginning, a middle, an end and one simile. (5 marks)
   Answer: A story of about 60 words in the past tense, with clear parts and a simile.
   Why: One mark for the beginning, one for events in order, one for the end, one for a simile, one for correct punctuation and past tense.
