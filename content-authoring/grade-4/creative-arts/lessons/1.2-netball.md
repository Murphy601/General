## PAGE 1: Netball, a team game
Netball is a team game played by two teams. Each team tries to score goals by throwing the ball through a ring at the top of a tall post. The ball is passed from player to player, and no one may run with it. Because of this, netball needs good passing, good catching and good teamwork.

A full team has seven players on the court. Younger learners play mini-netball, which has fewer players and a smaller court. Your teacher will tell you the numbers to use at school.

### The court
A full netball court is a rectangle 30.5 metres long and 15.25 metres wide. It is divided into three equal parts called thirds. There is a goal post at each end. The goal ring is 3.05 metres above the ground. Near each goal post there is a semicircle called the goal circle. Only players in the goal circle can shoot.

## PAGE 2: The seven positions
Each player wears a bib with letters that show her or his position. Each position has an area of the court where the player may go.

### Attackers (try to score)
• GS, Goal Shooter: stays in the goal third, including the goal circle, and shoots.
• GA, Goal Attack: plays in the centre third and the goal third, including the goal circle, and can shoot.
• WA, Wing Attack: plays in the centre third and the goal third but never in the goal circle. She feeds the ball to the shooters.

### Centre
• C, Centre: plays all over the court except the two goal circles. She takes the centre pass to start the game.

### Defenders (stop the other team)
• WD, Wing Defence: plays in the centre third and her own defending third but not in the goal circle.
• GD, Goal Defence: plays in the centre third and the defending third, including the goal circle.
• GK, Goal Keeper: stays in the defending third, including the goal circle, and tries to stop the shooter.

Only GS and GA may score goals, and only from inside the goal circle. Players must stay in their allowed areas. Stepping into a wrong area is a rule break.

### Fair play
A good player shakes hands with the other team at the end of the game, whether she has won or lost.

## PAGE 3: Catching with two hands
Before you can pass well, you must catch well. In netball, we learn the double-handed catch first, where both hands hold the ball.

### Steps for a double-handed catch
1. Watch the ball all the way from the thrower's hands into your own.
2. Move towards the ball and reach out with both arms, with elbows slightly bent.
3. Spread your fingers wide and make a shape like a window with your thumbs and first fingers, with the thumbs close together behind the ball.
4. When the ball touches your fingers, let your arms bend a little and pull the ball in towards your chest. This is called giving with the ball. It stops the ball bouncing out of your hands.
5. Stand with your knees slightly bent and feet apart so that you stay balanced.

### Common mistakes
• Looking at the next player before the ball is in your hands.
• Catching with stiff arms, so the ball bounces away.
• Catching with the palms and not the fingers.

### Practise in pairs
Stand about two metres apart. One partner rolls a ball gently or throws it under-arm. The other catches with two hands. Count how many catches you make in ten tries. Move a little further apart as you improve.

### Chest pass
1. Hold the ball with both hands at chest height, fingers spread, thumbs behind the ball.
2. Look at your partner's chest.
3. Step forward and push the ball out by straightening both arms. Your thumbs turn down as you let go.
4. The ball should travel fast and straight, and reach your partner at chest height.

### Overhead pass
1. Lift the ball above your head with both hands, elbows slightly bent.
2. Step forward and throw from behind the head, flicking your wrists and fingers.
3. The ball goes over a defender who is standing close to you.

Follow through with your fingers pointing at your partner. Use a chest pass to a player who is not marked closely, and an overhead pass when a defender is in the way.

## PAGE 5: Rules that make netball fair
Netball has rules so that every player has a fair chance and nobody gets hurt.

### Basic rules
• Do not run with the ball. When you catch the ball you may pivot, which means you keep one foot on the ground where you landed and turn on it, but you may not drag or hop on it.
• Pass or shoot within three seconds of getting the ball.
• Do not hold the ball for more than three seconds.
• Contact is not allowed. Do not push, trip, hold or grab another player.
• A defender must stand at least 0.9 metres, about a big step, away from the player with the ball.
• The ball must not be kicked, rolled or dropped and picked up again.
• The game starts with a centre pass, and again after each goal, with the teams taking turns.

### Umpires and signals
Umpires blow a whistle to stop the game for a rule break. Listen and follow their decisions without arguing.

### Safety
• Wear light sports clothes and shoes with good grip.
• Take off rings, bracelets and watches. Cut long nails.
• Warm up before playing and drink water afterwards.
• Play on a clear, flat surface with no stones or holes.

## PAGE 6: Making a ball with the overhand knot
Sometimes a school does not have enough balls. We can improvise, which means making something useful from materials we already have. A ball made from string and waste materials is a good example. The technique of tying knots to make patterns and shapes is called macramé.

### The overhand knot
The overhand knot is the simplest knot. It is the basic knot for this ball.

### Making the ball
Materials: a ball of crumpled paper or old cloth for the centre, sisal string, thin rope or strips of plastic bags, and scissors.
1. Make a firm core about the size of your fist. Wrap it with cloth or string so that it keeps its shape.
2. Cut eight cords of equal length, about as long as your arm.
3. Tie all the cords together in the middle with an overhand knot. Place the core on top of the knot.
4. Take neighbouring cords in pairs. Tie an overhand knot between each pair, all at the same height, so that the cords make a net around the core.
5. Make a second round of knots a little lower, using new pairs. Pull each knot tight.
6. Gather all the cords at the bottom of the core, and tie them tightly with a strong overhand knot. Trim the ends.

Tidy your place afterwards and keep the balls in a bag or a box.

## PAGE 7: Playing mini-netball
Now you can put your skills together in a mini-netball game. Mini-netball uses the same ideas as netball with fewer players and a smaller area. Your teacher may use a shorter goal post and change the number of players.

### Before the game
1. Warm up by jogging, stretching arms and legs, and doing a few passes in pairs.
2. Choose fair teams and positions. Let everyone have a turn in different positions.
3. Check that the playing area is clean and that the ball is firm.

### During the game
• Move into empty space so that a teammate can pass to you.
• Call your teammate's name or hold up a hand to show where you are.
• Pass early, before a defender arrives.
• Play your position and keep to the areas allowed.

### After the game
Thank the other team and the umpire. Talk about your own skills and your teammates' skills. Say one thing that went well, such as a good catch, and one thing to improve, such as a quicker pass.

Netball is a team game. A pass that gets a goal belongs to everyone who helped, not just to the shooter. Playing for enjoyment and fairness matters more than winning at any cost.

## QUIZ
1. How many players are in a full netball team on court?
   A) 5
   B) 6
   C) 7
   D) 9
   Answer: C
   Why: A full netball team has seven players. Mini-netball may use fewer.
2. Which two players are allowed to score goals?
   A) GK and GD
   B) GS and GA
   C) WA and C
   D) WD and GK
   Answer: B
   Why: Only the Goal Shooter and Goal Attack may shoot, from inside the goal circle.
3. Which action is against the rules of netball?
   A) Pivoting on your landing foot
   B) Passing within three seconds
   C) Running with the ball
   D) Catching with two hands
   Answer: C
   Why: Players may not run with the ball once they have caught it.
4. When should you use an overhead pass?
   A) To send the ball over a defender standing near you
   B) To kick the ball
   C) To bounce the ball to yourself
   D) To restart after lunch
   Answer: A
   Why: The ball is thrown from above the head so that it passes over the defender's arms.
5. What does giving with the ball mean when catching?
   A) Handing the ball to the umpire
   B) Bending your arms a little to take the force out of the ball
   C) Throwing the ball back at once
   D) Keeping your arms very stiff
   Answer: B
   Why: Bending the arms as the ball arrives softens the catch, so it does not bounce away.
6. Describe how to make a chest pass. (4 marks)
   Answer: Hold the ball with both hands at chest height with fingers spread. Look at your partner. Step forward and straighten your arms, turning your thumbs down as you release. The ball should reach your partner at chest height.
   Why: 1 mark for the holding position, 1 mark for looking at the target, 1 mark for step and push, 1 mark for the release and where the ball arrives.
7. State three rules that keep netball safe and fair. (3 marks)
   Answer: No contact such as pushing or tripping; no running with the ball; a defender must stay 0.9 metres away from the player with the ball. Other correct rules are accepted.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct rule.
8. Why do we improvise a ball with the macramé technique? (2 marks)
   Answer: The school may not have enough balls, and improvising lets us use materials we already have, such as string and waste cloth, so we can practise.
   Why: 1 mark for the need for a ball, 1 mark for using available materials.
9. Explain the steps of tying an overhand knot. (3 marks)
   Answer: Make a loop in the cord. Pass the end of the cord through the loop. Pull both ends to tighten the knot.
   Why: 1 mark for each step in the correct order.
