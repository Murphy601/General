## PAGE 1: What is a percussion instrument?
A percussion instrument makes sound when you hit it, shake it or scrape it. The word percussion comes from the idea of striking one thing against another. When you slap a drum with your palm, the drum skin shakes very fast, and we hear a sound. When you shake a gourd with seeds inside, the seeds knock against the walls and make a sound.

There are three main ways of playing percussion instruments.
• Striking: you hit the instrument with your hand or with a stick, for example a drum.
• Shaking: you move the instrument so that the things inside it rattle, for example a shaker.
• Scraping: you rub a stick along a rough surface, for example a notched piece of wood.

Kenya has many indigenous percussion instruments. Indigenous means that they belong to the people of a place and were made by them for a very long time, often from materials found nearby, such as wood, animal skin, gourds, reeds and seeds.

### Think and talk
Which percussion instruments have you seen or heard at a wedding, a church, a school music festival or on the radio? How was each one played? Was it struck, shaken or scraped?

## PAGE 2: Instruments from different Kenyan communities
To identify an instrument, we give three facts: its name, the community that plays it, and the way it is played.

### Ngoma (drum)
Drums are found in almost every Kenyan community. Ngoma is the Kiswahili word for drum, and also for a drumming dance. A drum is made from a hollow wooden body with animal skin stretched over the open end. It is played with the hands or with sticks.

### Isukuti
Isukuti is a lively dance of the Luhya people, especially the Isukha and Idakho. It is played with several drums of different sizes. The drummers hit them with sticks and hands, and dancers move to the beat at celebrations.

### Kayamba
The kayamba is a flat shaker from the Kenyan coast, used by the Mijikenda and other coastal people. It is made from reeds in a small frame, filled with dry seeds. You shake it back and forth, and it sounds like falling rain.

### Gourd rattle
A gourd rattle is made from a dried gourd with seeds or small stones inside. The player shakes it. In the Kikuyu community, a gourd rattle called gichandi is used in some performances.

Many other communities have their own drums, rattles and shakers. Ask an elder or a teacher which ones are played where you live.

### Parts and their functions
• Drumhead (skin): it shakes when struck and makes the sound.
• Shell (body): the hollow part. The air inside makes the sound louder and fuller.
• Rope or lacing: it holds the skin tight. A tighter skin gives a higher sound. A looser skin gives a lower sound.
• Beater (stick): it is used to strike the drumhead. Hands can also be used.

For a shaker or rattle, the parts are the container, the seeds inside and the handle. The seeds make the sound, and the container holds them.

## PAGE 4: Melodic and non-melodic percussion
Some percussion instruments can play different notes, high and low, so they can play a tune. We call them melodic instruments. Others make only one kind of sound, or sounds that do not form a clear tune. We call them non-melodic instruments.

### Melodic examples
• Xylophone: wooden bars of different lengths. Short bars give high notes and long bars give low notes.
• Thumb piano (kalimba or mbira): metal tongues of different lengths that you pluck with your thumbs.

### Non-melodic examples
• Most drums, played on their own, give a beat but not a tune.
• Shakers, rattles and kayamba.
• Claves (two wooden sticks hit together), tambourine and cowbell.

## PAGE 5: Improvising rhythmic patterns
A rhythm is a pattern of long and short sounds and silences. To improvise means to make something up on the spot, without writing it first. When you improvise a rhythmic pattern, you invent a pattern of beats using a drum, a shaker or another percussion instrument.

### How to improvise to a song
1. Sing the song softly and find the steady beat by tapping your knee.
2. Choose an instrument and play on every beat first. This is the pulse.
3. Change to a pattern. For example, play two quick sounds then one long sound: da-da-daa.
4. Repeat the pattern so that the singers can follow it.
5. Change the pattern at the end of a verse, so that the accompaniment does not become boring.

### Good group habits
• Play softly enough for the singers to be heard.
• Wait for your turn to try a solo.
• Keep the beat steady. Do not speed up when you feel excited.
• Different instruments can play different patterns at the same time. The mix sounds rich.

### Try it
Choose a song you all know. One group plays the pulse on drums. Another group shakes the kayamba on the first beat of every four. A third group improvises a short pattern. Perform together, then talk about what worked well.

## PAGE 6: Making charcoal sticks for drawing
Charcoal is black carbon that is left when wood is heated with very little air. A charcoal stick leaves a soft, dark mark on paper and is good for drawing.

Fire is dangerous. An adult or your teacher must do all the burning. Learners help with the cutting, trimming and cooling only when the adult says so.

### Materials
Dry, straight twigs as thick as a pencil, a knife used by an adult, an old metal tin with a lid, sand or water nearby, and tongs.

### Procedure
1. Cutting: cut straight twigs to about the length of your hand.
2. Trimming: peel off the bark and cut off knots and leaves so that the twigs are smooth.
3. Packing: pack the twigs tightly in the tin and close the lid. Pierce a small hole in the lid so that gas can escape.
4. Burning: the adult places the tin in a fire or on hot coals until the smoke from the hole stops. This takes a while.
5. Cooling: the adult removes the tin with tongs and leaves it closed until it is completely cold. Never open a hot tin, because fresh air can make the charcoal catch fire.

### Safety rules
• Keep at a safe distance from the fire.
• Never run near the fire. Tie back long clothes and hair.
• Collect only dead or fallen twigs. Do not cut living trees. This protects the environment.
• Wash your hands after touching charcoal.

## PAGE 7: Tonal value and the smudge technique

### Smudging
To smudge means to rub charcoal gently so that it spreads and blends.
1. Draw the outline of your percussion instrument lightly, for example a drum.
2. Shade the darkest parts, such as under the rim and on the side away from the light. Press harder for dark.
3. Shade lightly on the parts that face the light.
4. Use a finger, a tissue or a piece of cloth to rub the shading in small circles. The tones will blend smoothly.
5. Leave the brightest place as plain white paper, or lift charcoal off with a clean eraser.

Do not smudge with a wet finger. Keep your hands clean so that you do not stain the paper. Finish by showing your drawing and giving kind, honest comments on your friends' work.

## QUIZ
1. Which of these is a way of playing a percussion instrument?
   A) Blowing
   B) Plucking a string with a bow
   C) Striking
   D) Singing
   Answer: C
   Why: Percussion instruments are struck, shaken or scraped.
2. Which part of a drum makes the sound when it is struck?
   A) Drumhead
   B) Rope
   C) Handle
   D) Base stand
   Answer: A
   Why: The drumhead is the stretched skin. It vibrates and produces the sound.
3. What happens to the sound of a drum when the rope makes the skin tighter?
   A) The sound gets lower
   B) The sound gets higher
   C) The sound stops
   D) The sound does not change
   Answer: B
   Why: A tight skin vibrates faster, so the sound is higher.
4. Which instrument is melodic?
   A) Kayamba
   B) Claves
   C) Xylophone
   D) Gourd rattle
   Answer: C
   Why: A xylophone has bars of different lengths that give different notes, so it can play a tune.
5. What must never be done with a tin of hot charcoal sticks?
   A) Leave it closed to cool
   B) Open it while it is still hot
   C) Use tongs to move it
   D) Keep children away
   Answer: B
   Why: Fresh air can make hot charcoal catch fire, so the tin stays closed until it is cold.
6. Name two Kenyan percussion instruments and say how each is played. (3 marks)
   Answer: For example, the drum (ngoma) is struck with hands or sticks, and the kayamba is shaken.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct instrument and 1 mark for correct ways of playing both.
7. Explain the difference between a melodic and a non-melodic percussion instrument. (2 marks)
   Answer: A melodic instrument plays different notes and can play a tune, for example a xylophone. A non-melodic instrument gives a beat or a sound but no tune, for example a shaker.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct explanation with an example.
8. List the steps of making charcoal sticks in the correct order. (4 marks)
   Answer: Cut the twigs, trim off the bark, pack them in a tin with a small hole in the lid and burn them, then cool them in the closed tin.
   Why: 1 mark for each of the four stages in the right order: cutting, trimming, burning, cooling.
9. Describe how to make a smooth tonal change on a charcoal drawing using the smudge technique. (3 marks)
   Answer: Shade lightly with charcoal, pressing harder where it should be dark. Then rub with a finger or tissue so the tones blend from dark to light. Leave the brightest part white.
   Why: 1 mark for shading with different pressure, 1 mark for rubbing to blend, 1 mark for leaving a light area.
