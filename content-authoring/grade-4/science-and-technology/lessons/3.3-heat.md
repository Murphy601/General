## PAGE 1: What is heat?
On a cold morning, you rub your hands together and they feel warmer. When you touch a cup of hot tea, you feel something moving into your hand. That something is heat. Heat is a form of energy that makes things hot. When an object gets more heat, it becomes hotter. When it loses heat, it becomes cooler.

We can feel hot, warm and cold with our skin, but our skin can be fooled and we can be hurt. To measure how hot something is, we use a thermometer. The number it shows is called temperature and it is measured in degrees Celsius (°C). Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.

### Heat moves
Heat always moves from a hotter place to a cooler place. A cup of tea cools down because its heat goes into the air and the cup. An ice cube melts in your hand because heat goes from your hand into the ice.

With a partner, write in your own sentences what heat is and give three things that are hot and three that are cold. If you can, use a school computer or a book to find the meaning of heat and check your answer.

### Safety
Do not touch hot things to find out how hot they are. Use a thermometer or ask an adult.

## PAGE 2: Sources of heat
A source of heat is something that gives out heat. Let us look at the three main sources.

### The sun
The sun is our biggest natural source of heat. Its heat travels through space and warms the Earth. Without it the Earth would be very cold and there would be no life. The sun warms the land and the sea, dries wet clothes and makes plants grow.

### Fire
Burning gives out heat. People burn firewood, charcoal, paraffin and gas (in a cooking gas cylinder) to make fire. Fuel is a material that burns to give heat. A candle flame and a forest fire also give out heat. Fires can be dangerous.

### Electricity
When electricity passes through some appliances, they become hot. An electric kettle, an electric iron, a cooker, a heater and a hair dryer use electricity to make heat.

Rubbing also makes heat. Your hands get warm when you rub them because of friction.

## PAGE 3: Uses of heat
Heat is very useful. Every day people use it in many ways.

### Cooking
Heat cooks our food. It changes raw maize flour into ugali, raw rice into a soft meal and raw eggs into boiled eggs. Cooking also makes food safer to eat because heat kills many germs. We cook using firewood, charcoal, gas or electricity, or a solar cooker.

### Warming
Heat keeps us warm. In cold places like the highlands, people light fires or wear thick clothes. Heat also keeps chicks warm. A mother hen keeps her chicks warm under her wings, and farmers keep chicks warm with a lamp or heater in a brooder house.

### Ironing
A hot iron flattens wrinkles in clothes. The heat and the weight of the iron help make the cloth smooth. Irons can be electric or charcoal irons that have hot coal inside.

### Drying
Wet clothes dry faster in the sun. Farmers spread maize, beans and millet in the sun to dry, and fishermen dry fish in the sun. Heat turns the liquid water into a gas, which goes into the air. This is called evaporation.

### Other uses
Heat boils water to make it safe to drink. It is used to melt metals to make things such as sufurias. It is used to hatch eggs and to control pests.

## PAGE 4: Heat and materials
Some materials let heat pass through them easily. Others do not.

### Investigation: metal or wood?
Ask an adult to help you. Take a cup of warm water (not hot, and not boiling). Put a metal spoon in the cup and a wooden spoon in the cup. Wait for two minutes, then touch the top ends of the spoons. The metal spoon feels warmer. Metal lets heat travel along it quickly. Wood does not.

### Why this matters
Cooking pots are made of metal, because metal passes heat from the fire to the food. Handles are made of wood, plastic or thick cloth is used to hold pots, because these do not pass heat quickly and so they do not burn our hands. Kitchen gloves and cloths protect our hands from hot pots.

## PAGE 5: Staying safe with heat
Heat can burn skin, start fires and cause serious injuries. Follow these rules every time.

### Safety rules
• Do not play with matches, lighters or fire. Never light a fire without an adult.
• Keep small children away from fires, cookers and hot water.
• Use kitchen gloves or a thick cloth to hold hot pots, handles and trays.
• Turn the pot handles inwards so nobody knocks them over.
• Do not touch an electric iron, a cooker or a kettle while it is hot or on. Switch them off and let them cool.
• Do not touch electric sockets or wires with wet hands.
• Keep paraffin, petrol and gas away from fire and out of children's reach.
• Do not put paper, cloth or curtains near fire.
• Put out fire with sand or water only if an adult tells you, and call for help. Never use water on an oil fire.
• Never look directly at the sun.

### If someone is burnt
Put the burnt skin under cool running water for several minutes. Do not put butter, oil or toothpaste on it. Cover it with a clean cloth and tell an adult straight away. Take serious burns to a health worker quickly.

## PAGE 6: Why heat is important, and making kitchen gloves
### Importance of heat in daily life
• The sun's heat keeps the Earth warm enough for plants, animals and people to live.
• It makes the water cycle work. Heat from the sun turns water from seas and rivers into water vapour, which forms clouds and rain.
• It helps plants grow.
• It cooks our food and makes it safe.
• It dries clothes, crops and fish, so they last longer.
• It keeps us warm in cold weather.
• It is used in factories to make metals, bricks, glass and many other goods.

### Project: make kitchen gloves
Use locally available materials such as thick cotton cloth, old towels or sacking. You will also need a needle, thread, scissors and chalk.
1. Put your hand flat on the cloth and draw a mitten shape a little bigger than your hand with chalk.
2. Draw the same shape again. You need two pieces.
3. Cut both pieces out. Ask an adult to help with scissors.
4. Place a second layer of cloth inside each piece for extra thickness.
5. Sew the pieces together along the edges, leaving the wrist end open.
6. Turn it inside out, and test the glove by holding a warm (not hot) cup.
Thick layers of dry cloth pass heat slowly. Wet cloth passes heat quickly, so keep gloves dry.

### Common mistakes
• Saying heat and temperature are the same. Heat is energy. Temperature tells us how hot something is.
• Saying that cold moves into a warm object. Heat moves out of the warm object.
• Testing whether something is hot by touching it.
• Using wet cloths to lift hot pots.

## QUIZ
1. Which is the biggest natural source of heat for the Earth?
   A) The moon
   B) The sun
   C) A torch
   D) Stars at night
   Answer: B
   Why: The sun gives the Earth most of its heat.
2. Which of these gives out heat when electricity passes through it?
   A) A bicycle
   B) A wooden spoon
   C) An electric iron
   D) A ball
   Answer: C
   Why: An electric iron changes electricity into heat.
3. Why are cooking pot handles often made of wood or plastic?
   A) They are heavy
   B) They do not pass heat quickly so our hands do not burn
   C) They shine
   D) They are cheap to buy
   Answer: B
   Why: Wood and plastic are poor at passing heat, so the handle stays cooler than the metal pot.
4. What should you do first if you spill hot water on your arm?
   A) Rub butter on it
   B) Put it under cool running water and tell an adult
   C) Cover it with a dirty cloth
   D) Ignore it
   Answer: B
   Why: Cool running water reduces the burn. Butter, oil and dirt should not be put on a burn.
5. Wet clothes dry faster in the sun because
   A) heat from the sun turns the water into vapour
   B) the sun makes the cloth heavy
   C) the sun makes the water colder
   D) the sun makes the cloth thick
   Answer: A
   Why: Heat makes water evaporate, which turns it into a gas that goes into the air.
6. Name the three main sources of heat. (2 marks)
   Answer: The sun, fire and electricity.
   Why: 1 mark for two sources and 2 marks for all three.
7. State three uses of heat in daily life. (3 marks)
   Answer: Cooking, warming, ironing, drying (any three).
   Why: 1 mark for each correct use.
8. Give three safety rules to follow when using heat. (3 marks)
   Answer: Use gloves to hold hot pots, keep children away from the cooker, do not play with matches, turn pot handles inwards, switch off the iron.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct rule.
9. Explain why a metal spoon left in a cup of warm water feels warm at the top but a wooden spoon does not. (3 marks)
   Answer: Metal lets heat travel along it easily, so heat from the water reaches the top. Wood does not let heat pass easily, so the top stays cool.
   Why: 1 mark for metal passing heat, 1 mark for wood not passing heat, 1 mark for linking to the spoon results.
10. Explain why heat from the sun is important to life on Earth. (3 marks)
   Answer: It keeps the Earth warm, helps plants to grow, dries clothes and crops, and makes rain by evaporating water.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct point, up to 3.
