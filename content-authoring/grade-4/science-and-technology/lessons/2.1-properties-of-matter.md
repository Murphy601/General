## PAGE 1: What is matter?
Everything around you is made of something. Your desk, the water in your bottle, your uniform, the air in the classroom and even you are made of matter. Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. A bag of sugar has more mass than a small packet of salt. Taking up space means that an object needs room. A stone takes up the space where it sits, and no other stone can be in the same place at the same time.

### Matter or not matter?
• Matter: a book, milk, a football, smoke, air, sand.
• Not matter: light, sound, heat, a feeling such as happiness.

Light from a torch is not matter. It has no mass and it does not take up space.

### Is air matter?
Many pupils think air is nothing because we cannot see it. Test it. Blow up a balloon. The balloon gets bigger because the air you blew in takes up space inside it. Feel a wind that pushes a door. Air can push, so air is matter.

### Safety
Each pupil should use their own balloon. Do not blow air into a balloon that another child has already put in their mouth. Clean hands and clean balloons help to stop germs from spreading.

## PAGE 2: The three states of matter
Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid and gas. Everything you touch or breathe is in one of these states.

### Solids
A solid keeps its own shape. A stone, a book and a wooden spoon stay the same shape wherever you put them. A solid also has a fixed volume. Volume means the amount of space that something takes up. Solids can be hard like iron or soft like a sponge. Powders such as sugar, salt and flour are made of tiny solid grains. Each grain is a small solid.

### Liquids
A liquid has a fixed volume but no shape of its own. It takes the shape of the container it is in. Water, milk, cooking oil and juice are liquids. When you pour water from a bottle into a cup, the water changes shape but there is still the same amount of water.

### Gases
A gas has no fixed shape and no fixed volume. It spreads out to fill any container it is in. Air, the gas in a cooking cylinder, and the steam from a boiling pot are gases. Most gases are invisible. When you see white clouds above a boiling kettle, you are looking at tiny drops of water. The steam itself is invisible.

## PAGE 3: Comparing solids, liquids and gases
Now let us compare the three states using shape and volume.

Solid: has its own shape. Has a fixed volume. Cannot be poured (except powders).
Liquid: takes the shape of its container. Has a fixed volume. Can be poured.
Gas: fills the container it is in. Has no fixed volume. Can spread everywhere.

### Try it: shape of water
Get a bottle with 200 ml of water. Pour it into a tall glass. Then pour it into a wide bowl. The shape of the water changes, but the amount does not change. Now put a stone in the glass and in the bowl. The stone stays the same shape everywhere. This shows that a liquid takes the shape of its container and a solid keeps its shape.

### Try it: air takes up space
Crumple a piece of dry tissue paper and push it into the bottom of a dry plastic cup. Turn the cup upside down and push it straight down into a basin of water. Lift the cup straight up. The tissue is still dry, because the air trapped in the cup kept the water out. The air took up the space inside the cup.

## PAGE 4: Measuring mass and volume
Mass and volume are two properties we can measure.

### Measuring mass
Mass is measured with a balance. The units are the gram (g) and the kilogram (kg). 1 kg is 1000 g. Small things like a pencil are measured in grams. Sacks of maize or a bag of flour are measured in kilograms. A kilogram of sugar and a kilogram of stones have the same mass. But they do not take up the same amount of space.

### Measuring volume
The volume of a liquid is measured with a measuring jug or cylinder. The units are the millilitre (ml) and the litre (l). 1 l is 1000 ml. A cup holds about 250 ml. A big bottle of water may hold 1 l or 5 l. Read the scale at the level of the bottom of the curved surface of the water, with your eye at the same level.

## PAGE 5: Worked examples and a class investigation
### Worked example 1: mass
A shopkeeper puts 2 kg of rice and 500 g of beans in a bag. What is the total mass in grams?
Step 1: 2 kg = 2 x 1000 = 2000 g.
Step 2: Add 2000 g + 500 g = 2500 g.
Answer: 2500 g, which is 2 kg 500 g.

### Worked example 2: volume
A jug has 300 ml of water. Mary pours in another 450 ml. How much water is in the jug now?
Step 1: 300 ml + 450 ml = 750 ml.
Answer: 750 ml. The volume of the liquid has increased but the liquid still takes the shape of the jug.

### Investigation: group the substances
Collect ten items from your home or school. Sort them into three groups. Use a table in your book.
Solids: stone, chalk, sugar, spoon, pencil.
Liquids: water, cooking oil, milk, juice.
Gases: air in a balloon, gas in a cylinder, steam, air in a football.
Work in a team. Share the tasks. One person collects, one person writes, one person checks. Be careful not to taste unknown liquids.

### Digital help
Use a school tablet or computer if you have one to search for pictures of the three states of matter and check your groups.

## PAGE 6: Why the states of matter matter to us
Each state of matter is useful in daily life.

### Solids
We build houses with stones, bricks and wood. We make tools, chairs, clothes and shoes from solids. Solids keep their shape, so they can hold things up.

### Liquids
We drink water and milk. Liquids can be poured and carried in containers. Water is used for washing and cooking and for growing crops. Fuel like petrol and paraffin is a liquid. Oil helps machines to run smoothly.

### Gases
We breathe air, which contains oxygen. A cooking gas cylinder gives a flame for cooking. Gas in car and bicycle tyres carries the load. Balloons and footballs use air to keep their shape.

### Common mistakes
• Saying that sand is a liquid because it pours. Each grain of sand is a small solid.
• Saying that an empty bottle has nothing in it. It is full of air.
• Saying that mass and volume are the same thing. Mass is the amount of matter and volume is the space it takes up.
• Saying that steam is the white cloud we see. The white cloud is tiny drops of water. Steam is invisible.

### Summary
Matter has mass and takes up space. It exists as solid, liquid or gas. Solids keep their shape and volume, liquids keep their volume but take the shape of their container, and gases spread out to fill any container.

## QUIZ
1. Which of these is matter?
   A) Light from the sun
   B) Sound of a drum
   C) Air in a balloon
   D) Heat from a fire
   Answer: C
   Why: Air has mass and takes up space, so it is matter. Light, sound and heat are not matter.
2. Which state of matter takes the shape of the container it is in but has a fixed volume?
   A) Solid
   B) Liquid
   C) Gas
   D) All of them
   Answer: B
   Why: A liquid flows to take the shape of its container while its volume stays the same.
3. What instrument is used to measure the volume of a liquid?
   A) Beam balance
   B) Ruler
   C) Measuring jug
   D) Clock
   Answer: C
   Why: A measuring jug or cylinder has a scale in millilitres for measuring volume.
4. Which of these is a gas?
   A) Cooking oil
   B) Air in a football
   C) Milk
   D) Chalk
   Answer: B
   Why: Air is a gas. Cooking oil and milk are liquids and chalk is a solid.
5. How many grams are in 3 kg?
   A) 30 g
   B) 300 g
   C) 3000 g
   D) 30 000 g
   Answer: C
   Why: 1 kg is 1000 g, so 3 kg = 3 x 1000 = 3000 g.
6. What is matter? Give two examples of matter. (2 marks)
   Answer: Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Examples are a stone, water and air.
   Why: 1 mark for the meaning and 1 mark for two correct examples.
7. Write the shape and volume properties of solids and liquids. (3 marks)
   Answer: A solid has its own shape and a fixed volume. A liquid has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of the container, but its volume is fixed.
   Why: 1 mark for solid properties, 1 mark for liquid shape and 1 mark for liquid volume.
8. Chebet says that the air in an empty bottle does not exist because she cannot see it. Describe one simple activity that shows her that air is matter. (3 marks)
   Answer: Push an upside-down cup with a dry tissue in it into water. The tissue stays dry because air takes up the space in the cup. (Blowing up a balloon also works.)
   Why: 1 mark for a suitable activity, 1 mark for the observation, 1 mark for the conclusion that air takes up space.
9. A jug holds 250 ml of water. Musa adds 500 ml. How much water is in the jug? Then he adds 1 litre more. Give the final answer in ml. (3 marks)
   Answer: 250 + 500 = 750 ml, then 750 + 1000 = 1750 ml.
   Why: 1 mark for 750 ml, 1 mark for changing 1 l to 1000 ml, 1 mark for 1750 ml.
10. Give two ways liquids and two ways gases are useful in our daily life. (4 marks)
   Answer: Liquids: drinking water, washing, cooking, fuels. Gases: air for breathing, cooking gas, gas in tyres.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct use, up to 2 for liquids and 2 for gases.
