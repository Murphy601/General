## PAGE 1: What is solid waste?
Every day we throw things away. Banana peels, broken bottles, empty juice packets, old shoes and used batteries are all solid waste. Solid waste is any unwanted solid material that we throw away. It is different from liquid waste, such as dirty water.

Waste is made at home, at school, in the market, at shops and at big events such as weddings, funerals, sports days and church or mosque gatherings. Big towns like Nairobi, Kisumu and Mombasa make tonnes of waste every day. When waste is not managed well, it can harm people, animals and the environment.

### Walk and find
Take a walk around your school compound with a teacher. Do not touch waste with bare hands. Look and write down the different kinds of waste that you can see. You may find leaves, food remains, paper, plastic bottles, polythene bags, sweet wrappers and tins.

### Where does waste come from?
• The kitchen: vegetable peelings, food leftovers, bones, egg shells.
• The classroom: paper, pencil shavings, broken pens, chalk dust.
• The farm: dung, crop remains, old sacks.
• Homes and shops: bottles, tins, packaging, old clothes.
• Electronics: old phones, radios and batteries. This is called e-waste.

## PAGE 2: Decomposable and non-decomposable waste
Some waste rots and turns into soil after some time. This is decomposable waste. It is also called biodegradable waste. Small living things called decomposers, such as fungi and bacteria, break it down. Animals like earthworms help too.

Some waste does not rot, or takes a very long time to break down. This is non-decomposable waste. It stays in the environment for many years.

### Examples
Decomposable: kitchen waste, fruit peels, leaves, dung, paper (it rots slowly), cotton cloth.
Non-decomposable: plastics, metals such as tins and old nails, glass, food wrappers made of plastic, rubber, e-waste.

### Careful
Paper rots faster than plastic, but it is still slower than a banana peel. A banana peel rots in weeks. Glass and plastic bottles stay for hundreds of years or longer.

## PAGE 3: Dangers of solid waste
Waste is only a danger when we leave it lying around. Here are the main dangers.

### Danger to health
Rotting waste has a bad smell. It attracts flies, cockroaches and rats. These carry germs that cause diseases such as diarrhoea, typhoid and cholera. Old tins and tyres that collect rainwater give mosquitoes a place to lay eggs, and mosquitoes spread malaria.

### Danger to living things
Animals like goats and cows may eat polythene bags and get sick or die. Wild animals and birds can be trapped in plastic. Fish are harmed when plastic waste is washed into rivers, lakes and the ocean.

### Danger to the environment
• Polythene bags and plastic block drains and cause floods when it rains.
• Plastics stay in the soil for many years and stop water and roots from passing.
• Waste in rivers makes the water dirty, so it may not be safe to drink.
• Burning waste makes smoke that is bad for breathing. Burning plastic makes especially harmful smoke.
• Old batteries and other e-waste have harmful chemicals that can leak into the soil and water.

### Danger of injury
Broken glass, nails and sharp tin lids can cut people and animals. Cuts from dirty metal can get infected.

## PAGE 4: Managing waste: reduce, reuse, recycle

### Reduce
Make less waste. Take only the food you can eat. Buy things with less packaging. Carry a cloth or reusable bag to the market rather than taking a new polythene bag each time.

### Reuse
Use things again. Wash a plastic bottle and use it for carrying water. Use an empty tin as a pencil holder or as a flower pot. Use old clothes as cleaning cloths or pass them on.

### Recycle
Turn waste into new things. Old plastic can be melted and made into new plastic items. Paper is turned into new paper. Metal such as aluminium cans and scrap metal is melted and made into new items. Recycling needs collecting and sorting waste first.

### Composting
Decomposable waste can be put in a compost pit with dry leaves and some soil. After some weeks it turns into compost, a dark soil-like material that helps crops to grow.

## PAGE 5: Managing waste in different places
Each place needs simple rules to keep waste under control.

### In the classroom
• Have a bin with a lid or a box for waste, and use it.
• Put used paper in a separate box for reusing or recycling.
• Do not throw wrappers on the floor or out of the windows.

### At school
• Put bins in the compound, near the kitchen and at the playground.
• Use different bins for food waste and plastic if you can.
• Choose class monitors to check the bins are emptied and clean.
• Dig a compost pit or a rubbish pit far from the classrooms and kitchen.

### At home
• Sort waste into kitchen waste, paper, plastic, glass and metal.
• Feed vegetable peelings to livestock or put them in a compost pit.
• Take the rest to the collection point or the dump site. Do not burn plastic.

### At public functions
• Bring bins to the place before people arrive.
• Ask the organisers to use fewer disposable plates and cups.
• Clean the place after the event.

### Worked example
A school kitchen throws away vegetable leaves, empty oil tins, and maize sacks. Where should they go? The leaves go into the compost pit. The tins can be cleaned and reused, or taken to a scrap metal dealer. The sacks can be reused to carry things or turned into mats.

## PAGE 6: Staying safe and making useful things
### Safety when handling waste
• Wear gloves and closed shoes or gumboots.
• Use a rake, a shovel or a stick to pick up waste. Do not use bare hands.
• Do not touch sharp things like broken glass, needles or syringes. Tell a teacher.
• Wash your hands with soap and clean water after handling waste.
• Never burn waste in a place where children are playing.
• Do not taste, smell or play with waste.

### Project 1: make a dustbin
Get a big plastic bucket with a hole in it, a strong cardboard box or an old water container with a wide top. Cut the top if needed. Cover the sharp edges with tape. Paint a label such as "Waste only". Put it in the classroom and use it.

### Project 2: make toys or ornaments
Use waste bottle tops, old cloth, cardboard, tins and paper. You can make a toy car from a box and four bottle tops as wheels, or beads from rolled paper. Ask an adult when you need to cut. Keep your work clean and safe.

### Remember
The best waste is the waste that is never made. First reduce, then reuse, then recycle. Never leave waste on the ground.

## QUIZ
1. Which of these is decomposable waste?
   A) Plastic bottle
   B) Banana peel
   C) Tin can
   D) Glass bottle
   Answer: B
   Why: A banana peel is rotted by decomposers and turns into soil. The others do not rot easily.
2. Which of these is a danger of throwing waste anywhere?
   A) Blocked drains and floods
   B) Cleaner rivers
   C) More fresh air
   D) More compost
   Answer: A
   Why: Polythene and plastic waste block drains, and water then floods the area.
3. Turning old plastic into new plastic items is called what?
   A) Reducing
   B) Reusing
   C) Recycling
   D) Composting
   Answer: C
   Why: Recycling means making new things from waste materials.
4. Which of these is the best way to reduce waste?
   A) Buying more packaged items
   B) Carrying a reusable bag to the market
   C) Burning plastic
   D) Throwing bottles in the river
   Answer: B
   Why: A reusable bag means you do not need to take a new bag every time.
5. What should you wear when collecting waste?
   A) Sandals
   B) Bare feet
   C) Gloves and closed shoes
   D) Only a hat
   Answer: C
   Why: Gloves and shoes protect you from cuts, germs and sharp objects.
6. Give two examples of decomposable waste and two of non-decomposable waste. (2 marks)
   Answer: Decomposable: vegetable peelings, dung. Non-decomposable: plastic, tins, glass (any two of each).
   Why: 1 mark for two correct decomposable examples and 1 mark for two correct non-decomposable examples.
7. Explain how waste tins that hold rainwater can be a danger to health. (2 marks)
   Answer: Mosquitoes lay eggs in the standing water and spread malaria.
   Why: 1 mark for mosquitoes breeding in the water and 1 mark for malaria.
8. State the meaning of reduce, reuse and recycle with one example each. (3 marks)
   Answer: Reduce means to make less waste, for example carrying a cloth bag. Reuse means to use again, for example using a bottle to carry water. Recycle means to make waste into new things, for example paper made into new paper.
   Why: 1 mark for each correct meaning with an example.
9. Your school has a lot of waste on the compound. Suggest three things pupils can do to keep it clean. (3 marks)
   Answer: Put waste in bins, sort waste, make a compost pit, make monitors to check the bins, reuse bottles.
   Why: 1 mark for each sensible action, up to 3.
10. Explain why burning plastic waste is not a good way of getting rid of it. (2 marks)
   Answer: It makes harmful smoke that is bad for people and the air.
   Why: 1 mark for harmful smoke and 1 mark for effect on breathing or the environment.
