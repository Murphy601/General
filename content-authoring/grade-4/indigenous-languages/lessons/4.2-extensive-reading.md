## PAGE 1: Reading widely for pleasure
Extensive reading means reading many books, stories and articles for enjoyment and general understanding. You do not stop at every word or answer questions after every page. You read because you want to.

The more you read, the more words you know, the better you spell and the more ideas you gather. Readers become better writers and speakers.

### Reading silently
Silent reading means reading with your eyes, without saying the words aloud. It is faster, and it lets you think about what you read. It is also polite, because you do not disturb others in a library or a classroom. You can read silently anywhere.

### Two key questions
• Why do we read silently?
• What makes a book interesting to read?
An interesting book may have a good story, funny or brave characters, pictures, and words that create pictures in your mind.

### Level readers
Level readers are books written for a particular reading level. Grade 4 readers use words and sentence lengths suitable for pupils your age. In your mother tongue, there may be a few readers. Your class can gather them, together with stories collected from elders, to make a class library.

## PAGE 2: Building a class library
A class library is a small collection of books, stories and magazines kept in one place, such as a shelf or a box.

### How to start
1. Collect books from home, school and the community. Include English and Kiswahili books and any in your mother tongue.
2. Ask elders to tell stories, and write them out in neat handwriting to make your own class books.
3. Cover the books to protect them.
4. Write the title and the owner's name inside.
5. Keep a borrowing list: who took which book and when.
6. Choose two pupils each week as librarians.

### Rules for handling books
• Wash and dry your hands before you read.
• Turn pages gently.
• Never write in a library book.
• Return books on time.
• Report any damage honestly.

### Reading club
A reading club is a group of pupils who meet regularly to share what they read. Members recommend books, read aloud to each other and discuss the stories. Your club can choose a name and a motto.

## PAGE 3: Vocabulary from readers
Every book gives you new words. Good readers notice them and learn them.

### Steps
1. As you read, do not stop for every word. Note the ones that appear again and again.
2. After reading, list the words on a page.
3. Guess the meaning from the sentences near the word.
4. Check in a simple dictionary. A dictionary shows words in alphabetical order.
5. Write a sentence of your own using the word.

### Example
"The farmer was weary after a long day in the shamba." Clues: a long day, working hard. Weary means very tired.

### Code word and crossword puzzles
Make a simple puzzle with your vocabulary.
A code word puzzle gives every letter a number, and pupils fill in the letters to make words. A crossword gives clues, such as "Very tired (5 letters)", and the answers cross each other.
Example clue: "A person who grows crops" (6 letters). Answer: farmer.

Make a puzzle with a partner using ten words from your reader. Swap with another pair to solve it.

## PAGE 4: What are proverbs and sayings?
A proverb is a short, wise saying that gives advice or tells a truth about life. A saying is a common phrase that people use to express an idea. Proverbs are usually passed down from elders. They are part of oral literature, though they are also often written in books.

### Examples in English
• "A stitch in time saves nine." (Fix a small problem early to avoid a bigger one.)
• "Many hands make light work." (A job is easier when people share it.)
• "Look before you leap." (Think before you act.)
• "Honesty is the best policy." (It is best to tell the truth.)

### Examples in Kiswahili
• "Haba na haba hujaza kibaba." Meaning: little by little fills the measure. Small savings add up.
• "Asiyesikia la mkuu huvunjika guu." Meaning: one who does not listen to the elders may get hurt.
• "Kidole kimoja hakivunji chawa." Meaning: one finger cannot crush a louse; we need to work together.

### Your community's proverbs
Every community has proverbs. Ask elders for the proverbs of your people. Write them in your language, then explain the meaning in your own words.

## PAGE 5: Finding the meaning of proverbs
Proverbs often mean more than their words say. To understand one, do not stop at the words. Ask what lesson it teaches.

### Steps to explain a proverb
1. Read or say the proverb slowly.
2. Picture what the words show.
3. Ask: what lesson does this teach about life?
4. Think of a real situation where it fits.
5. Explain it in your own words.

### Example
"Many hands make light work."
Picture: many people carrying one heavy load.
Lesson: when we share work, the job is easier.
Situation: the whole class cleans the compound in ten minutes instead of one pupil taking an hour.

### Proverb game
Write proverbs on strips and their meanings on other strips. Mix them. In groups, match each proverb with its meaning. The first group to match all correctly wins.

### Connect to school rules
"Asiyesikia la mkuu huvunjika guu" is like obeying school rules. Rules keep us safe. Ignoring them may bring trouble. Find another proverb that fits a school rule and explain how.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
Extensive reading builds vocabulary and love for reading. A class library and a reading club give many books to enjoy. Proverbs are short wise sayings that teach lessons, and we understand them by finding the meaning behind the words.

### Remember
• Read silently and widely.
• Look after books.
• Learn new words in context.
• A proverb has a lesson beyond its words.

### Common mistakes
• Reading aloud in a library.
• Thinking that a proverb should be taken word for word.
• Turning proverbs from your language into English word for word without checking the meaning with an elder.
• Losing or damaging borrowed books.

### Final activity
Choose a book you enjoyed. Tell your partner: the title, one new word and its meaning, and one proverb that fits the story. Then say why you would or would not recommend the book.

## QUIZ
1. What is extensive reading?
   A) Reading many books for pleasure and general understanding
   B) Reading one line many times
   C) Copying a book
   D) Reading only the title
   Answer: A
   Why: Extensive reading means reading widely for enjoyment.
2. What is a proverb?
   A) A short wise saying that gives advice
   B) A long story
   C) A type of dictionary
   D) A kind of puzzle
   Answer: A
   Why: Proverbs are short sayings that teach a truth or give advice.
3. What does the proverb Many hands make light work teach?
   A) Everyone should keep their hands clean
   B) Work is easier when people share it
   C) Hands are heavy
   D) Work should be done alone
   Answer: B
   Why: When many people help, the work is lighter.
4. In the sentence, The farmer was weary after a long day in the shamba, what does weary most likely mean?
   A) Very tired
   B) Very happy
   C) Very hungry
   D) Very young
   Answer: A
   Why: A long day of work in the shamba suggests tiredness.
5. Give two rules for looking after library books. (2 marks)
   Answer: Turn pages gently, never write in the books, and return them on time.
   Why: One mark for each sensible rule.
6. Explain what the Kiswahili proverb Haba na haba hujaza kibaba means. (2 marks)
   Answer: Little by little fills the measure. Small amounts add up to a lot.
   Why: One mark for the literal meaning and one for the lesson.
7. List the steps you would follow to explain a proverb. (3 marks)
   Answer: Say it slowly, picture what it shows, decide what lesson it teaches, think of a real situation where it fits, and explain in your own words.
   Why: One mark for each step, up to three.
8. Why is it better to read silently in a library? (2 marks)
   Answer: It does not disturb others and it lets you think about what you read.
   Why: One mark for each reason.
