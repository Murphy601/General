## PAGE 1: Oral narratives
An oral narrative is a story that is told aloud, not read from a book. In every community, grandparents, parents and elders tell stories in the evening, at ceremonies or during long journeys. Some are made-up tales, often about animals that talk and behave like people. Some tell about the community's past. Many teach about right and wrong.

Oral narratives were passed from one generation to the next by word of mouth. That is why we call this kind of literature oral. The storyteller uses voice, face and hands to make the story come alive, and the listeners often join in with a song or a repeated line.

### Parts of an oral narrative
• Opening: a way of beginning that tells the audience a story is coming.
• Characters: the people or animals in the story.
• Events: what happens, in order.
• Ending: how the story finishes.
• Lesson: what we learn.

### Key question
Why is it important to speak fluently when telling a story? Because a smooth, clear voice keeps listeners interested and helps them follow. Ask an elder to tell you a story from your community and notice how they begin and end.

## PAGE 2: Telling a story well
A good storyteller practises. Here are steps for reciting or telling a story fluently and with clear pronunciation.

1. Know the story well. Tell it to yourself first.
2. Break it into parts: beginning, middle and end.
3. Speak each word clearly. Pronunciation means the way we say the sounds of words. Say the ends of words as well as the beginnings.
4. Use a steady pace, neither too fast nor too slow.
5. Change your voice for different characters and feelings.
6. Look at your audience.
7. End with a clear closing, and say the lesson.

### A model story in English
Your class can retell it in your mother tongue or make a similar one.
"A hare and a tortoise were neighbours. The hare boasted that he was the fastest animal. The tortoise said, 'Let us race.' The hare ran quickly, then lay down to sleep. The tortoise walked slowly and steadily and reached the end first. The lesson: slow and steady wins the race."

### Tongue twisters help pronunciation
A tongue twister is a phrase that is hard to say fast, such as "She sells sea shells." Practising them helps us say sounds clearly. Ask elders for tongue twisters in your own language.

## PAGE 3: Similes
A simile compares two things using the words like or as. It helps listeners see a picture in their minds.

### Examples in English
• "as quick as lightning"
• "as brave as a lion"
• "as light as a feather"
• "The baby slept like a log."
• "Her smile was as bright as the sun."

### How a simile works
Look at "as tall as a tree". We are comparing a tall person to a tree. The word as (or like) is the signal.

### Make your own
Complete: 1. The road was as long as ______. 2. The teacher's voice was like ______. 3. The mango was as sweet as ______.
Many answers are possible, such as a snake, thunder, honey.

### Similes in your community
Every language has its own similes drawn from the things people know: animals, weather, farming and daily life. Ask your parents, grandparents and neighbours for sayings that compare one thing to another. Write them in your mother tongue, and explain the meaning in English or in your mother tongue. Do not translate word for word, because the meaning may change. Instead, say in your own words what the comparison shows.

## PAGE 4: Metaphors
A metaphor says that one thing is another thing. It does not use like or as. It is a stronger, shorter comparison.

### Examples in English
• "Time is money."
• "My brother is a lion at football." (He plays bravely.)
• "The classroom was a beehive." (It was busy and noisy.)
• "She is a shining star." (She is very good or special.)

### Telling them apart
If you see like or as comparing two different things, it is a simile. If one thing is said to be another, it is a metaphor. A metaphor is not meant to be taken word for word. The classroom is not really a beehive.

### Discuss meaning
Talk with a partner: what does "The teacher is a walking dictionary" mean? Answer: the teacher knows a lot of words.

## PAGE 5: Using similes and metaphors in speech
Similes and metaphors make speaking colourful. They should be used with care so that they are clear and kind.

### Guidelines
• Choose a comparison your listeners will understand.
• Make sure it fits the thing you describe.
• Do not use comparisons that insult or hurt someone.
• Do not use too many in one sentence.

### Practice sentences on school rules
1. Obeying school rules is like following a map. (simile: it guides us)
2. Rules are the fence that protects the school garden. (metaphor)
3. A good pupil is as punctual as the school bell. (simile)

### Wall charts
In a group, collect similes and metaphors from your community. On a wall chart, make three columns: the saying, the type (simile or metaphor) and the meaning. Display it for a gallery walk. Classmates read and add helpful comments on sticky notes.

### Compose an oral narrative
Compose a short story on a school rule, such as coming on time or keeping the school clean. Include at least one simile and one metaphor. Practise and stage it in class in your mother tongue.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
Oral narratives are stories told aloud that entertain and teach. A fluent teller uses clear pronunciation, steady pace and voice change. A simile compares using like or as. A metaphor says one thing is another.

### Remember
• Practise the story before you tell it.
• Speak each word clearly.
• Simile: like or as. Metaphor: is.
• Explain meaning in your own words.

### Common mistakes
• Telling the story too fast.
• Forgetting the ending or the lesson.
• Taking a metaphor word for word.
• Translating a saying from your language word for word into English, which may lose its meaning.
• Using comparisons that hurt others.

### Final activity
Think of a person you admire. Say one simile and one metaphor about them, such as "She is as kind as rain in a dry season" and "My grandfather is a library." Ask your partner to say which is which.

## QUIZ
1. What is an oral narrative?
   A) A story told aloud
   B) A written test
   C) A drawing
   D) A list of names
   Answer: A
   Why: Oral means spoken, and a narrative is a story.
2. Which sentence is a simile?
   A) Time is money.
   B) The teacher is a walking dictionary.
   C) He ran as fast as the wind.
   D) The classroom was a beehive.
   Answer: C
   Why: It compares using the word as. The others are metaphors.
3. Which sentence is a metaphor?
   A) She sings like a bird.
   B) He is as strong as an ox.
   C) The baby slept like a log.
   D) My mother is a rock.
   Answer: D
   Why: It says the mother is a rock without using like or as.
4. What does pronunciation mean?
   A) The way we say the sounds of words
   B) The way we spell words
   C) The meaning of words
   D) The number of words
   Answer: A
   Why: Pronunciation is the manner of saying words, including their sounds.
5. Name three parts of an oral narrative. (3 marks)
   Answer: Opening, characters, events, ending, lesson (any three).
   Why: One mark for each correct part.
6. Complete with a simile: The mango was as sweet as ______. (2 marks)
   Answer: honey (or sugar or any sensible comparison)
   Why: One mark for a sensible comparison and one for keeping the pattern as sweet as.
7. Explain the difference between a simile and a metaphor, with one example of each. (4 marks)
   Answer: A simile compares using like or as, for example as quick as lightning. A metaphor says one thing is another, for example, Time is money.
   Why: One mark each for the two explanations and one mark each for the two examples.
8. Give two guidelines for using similes and metaphors when speaking. (2 marks)
   Answer: Use comparisons the listeners understand, and do not use comparisons that hurt or insult anyone.
   Why: One mark for each sensible guideline.
