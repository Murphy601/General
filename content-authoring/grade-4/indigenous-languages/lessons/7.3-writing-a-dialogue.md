## PAGE 1: What is a written dialogue?
A dialogue is a conversation between two or more people. When we write a dialogue, we show exactly what each person says. Dialogues appear in plays, stories, comics and film scripts.

Writing a dialogue is a creative task. You invent characters and decide what they say, how they feel and what happens.

### Why write dialogues?
• They make stories come alive.
• They show how characters think and feel.
• They let us practise how people really talk.
• They can teach values such as respect, honesty and how to spend free time well.

### Key question
Why are interjections important in a dialogue? They show feelings quickly and make conversation sound natural. Without them a dialogue can sound stiff.

### Model in English
Amina: Hurray! It is Saturday.
Otieno: Wow! What shall we do today?
Amina: Let us play football.
Otieno: Oh no! I have to fetch water first.
Amina: I will help you. Then we can play together.
Otieno: Thank you, Amina. You are a good friend.

### Look closely
Each speaker has a name followed by a colon. Each line is what that person says. Some lines start with an interjection.

## PAGE 2: Steps for writing a dialogue
Follow these steps.

1. Choose the theme. For example, how to spend leisure time.
2. Choose two or three characters. Give them names.
3. Decide the setting: where and when they are talking.
4. Decide the problem or purpose of the conversation.
5. Write each speaker's name, then a colon, then the words.
6. Start a new line every time a new person speaks.
7. Use a variety of sentences: statements, questions and commands.
8. Add interjections to show feelings.
9. Give the dialogue an ending.
10. Read it aloud with a partner to check that it sounds natural.

### Tip
Keep each speaker's line short. In real conversations, people do not give long speeches.

### Set-up example
Theme: Using free time well.
Characters: Baraka and Neema.
Setting: A school field after lessons.
Problem: Baraka wants to watch television, but Neema invites him to read.
Ending: Baraka agrees to try a book.

## PAGE 3: Interjections in your community
Interjections in your own language will make your dialogue authentic. They may be single sounds or short words used to show surprise, joy, pain, worry and other feelings.

### Collect them
1. Ask elders and friends which short expressions they use for each feeling.
2. Write each on a flashcard with the feeling on the back.
3. Sort the cards by feeling.
4. Check spellings with your teacher.

### Do not invent
A written language can be damaged when people make up spellings that do not match how the sounds are truly written. If you are not sure how to write an interjection, ask your teacher or a person who writes the language.

### A chart
Make a chart of four columns: Feeling, Interjection in English, Interjection in my community, When it is used. Fill in as many rows as you can.

Joy: Hurray
Pain: Ouch
Surprise: Wow
Disappointment: Oh no

Your community's expressions go in the third column. You will use this chart when you write.

### Rules
• Write the speaker's name at the start of each line, then a colon.
• Begin the spoken words with a capital letter.
• End with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.
• An interjection at the start is followed by an exclamation mark for strong feelings or a comma for mild ones.
• Start a new line for each speaker.

### Stage directions
A stage direction tells the actor how to speak or move. It goes in brackets.
Amina: (smiling) Hurray! It is Saturday.
Otieno: (frowning) Oh no! I forgot my homework.
Use them only when they help.

## PAGE 5: Writing, sharing and acting
Now write your own dialogue.

### Plan
Theme: How to spend leisure time.
Characters: two friends.
Setting: after school.
Problem: One wants to play, the other must first finish a task.

### Model dialogue
Wanjiku: Wow! The sun is shining. Let us go to the field.
Baraka: (sadly) Oh no! I must first wash the dishes.
Wanjiku: Do not worry. I will help you.
Baraka: Hurray! Then we can play sooner.
Wanjiku: Yes, many hands make light work.
Baraka: Thank you, my friend.

### Share and revise
Read your dialogue with a partner. Ask:
• Does each speaker sound different?
• Are the interjections suitable?
• Are the names and colons correct?
Fix any mistakes.

### Role play
Act your dialogue in a small group. Use your voice and face to show feelings. Perform in your mother tongue if your teacher allows and you have checked the writing.

### A singing game with interjections
Compose a short singing game with a partner. Choose a simple tune you know. Use interjections in the song, such as calling out surprise or joy at the end of each line. Teach it to the class.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
A dialogue shows a conversation between characters. To write one, choose a theme, characters and setting, write each speaker on a new line, punctuate carefully and include suitable interjections. Then read, revise and perform it.

### Remember
• Speaker name, colon, spoken words.
• A new line for each speaker.
• Interjections show feelings.
• Read it aloud to check.

### Common mistakes
• Writing long speeches instead of short lines.
• Forgetting the colon after the name.
• Using too many interjections in one line.
• Not giving the dialogue an ending.
• Writing the speaker's name in the middle of the words.

### Final activity
Write a six-line dialogue between two friends who plan how to use their leisure time. Include at least two interjections and one question. Swap with a partner and read both dialogues aloud.

## QUIZ
1. What is a dialogue?
   A) A conversation between two or more people
   B) A long description
   C) A list of foods
   D) A map
   Answer: A
   Why: A dialogue records the words of people talking to each other.
2. What comes straight after a speaker's name in a script dialogue?
   A) A colon
   B) A full stop
   C) A bracket
   D) A number
   Answer: A
   Why: The speaker's name is followed by a colon and then the words.
3. Why do writers put interjections in dialogues?
   A) To show feelings and make speech sound natural
   B) To make the page longer
   C) To hide the story
   D) To show the title
   Answer: A
   Why: Interjections show quick emotions.
4. What is the purpose of a stage direction?
   A) To tell the actor how to speak or move
   B) To end the story
   C) To name the author
   D) To count lines
   Answer: A
   Why: Stage directions guide actors and are placed in brackets.
5. Write the first two lines of a dialogue between Neema and Juma about playing after school. Use one interjection. (3 marks)
   Answer: Neema: Hurray! School has ended. Juma: Let us play in the field. (Any two correct lines with names, colons and one interjection.)
   Why: One mark for names and colons, one for an interjection, one for suitable content.
6. List four steps for writing a dialogue. (4 marks)
   Answer: Choose a theme, choose characters, decide the setting, write each speaker's name with a colon and words, use interjections, read it aloud.
   Why: One mark for each correct step, up to four.
7. Why should you ask an elder or teacher about writing interjections in your mother tongue? (2 marks)
   Answer: To make sure the spelling and meaning are correct, and not invented.
   Why: One mark for correct spelling and one for correct meaning.
8. Explain two mistakes to avoid when writing a dialogue. (2 marks)
   Answer: Do not write long speeches and do not forget the colon after each name.
   Why: One mark for each valid mistake.
