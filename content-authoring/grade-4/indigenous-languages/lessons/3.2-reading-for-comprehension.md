## PAGE 1: What is a dialogue?
A dialogue is a conversation between two or more people. When you read a dialogue, you see who is speaking and what each person says. Dialogues appear in plays, stories and textbooks.

In a written dialogue, the name of the speaker usually comes first, followed by a colon, and then the words spoken. Look at this model dialogue in English about personal safety. Your teacher may ask you to change it into your mother tongue.

Teacher: Good morning, class. Today we shall talk about staying safe on the road.
Wambui: Teacher, should we cross anywhere?
Teacher: No, Wambui. We should cross at a zebra crossing, if there is one.
Baraka: What should we do first?
Teacher: First look right, then left, then right again. Cross only when the road is clear.

### Notice
Each line starts with the name of the speaker. Questions have question marks. Each speaker takes a turn.

### Try it
Read the dialogue aloud with two friends. One pupil reads Teacher, one reads Wambui and one reads Baraka.

## PAGE 2: Reading a dialogue well
To read a dialogue fluently, follow these steps.

1. Read silently to understand the whole conversation.
2. Choose your part. Underline your lines with a pencil.
3. Practise your lines aloud, using the right feeling.
4. Watch for the punctuation. A question mark means your voice goes up. An exclamation mark shows surprise or strong feeling.
5. Listen to the other speakers and come in at the right moment.

### Taking turns
In a dialogue, pupils take turns. Never read another person's lines unless you have swapped parts. Wait for your cue, which is the last few words of the person before you.

### Comprehension questions
After reading, answer questions from the dialogue above.
1. What is the topic? (staying safe on the road)
2. Where should pupils cross? (at a zebra crossing)
3. In which order do you look? (right, left, right)
4. When should you cross? (when the road is clear)

Always go back to the dialogue to check your answers.

## PAGE 3: Vocabulary from the dialogue
Some words in a dialogue may be new to you. Let us look at the words in the road safety dialogue: safe, cross, zebra crossing, clear, traffic.

### Steps for learning new words
1. Underline the new word.
2. Read the sentence before and after it.
3. Guess the meaning.
4. Check with a friend or a dictionary.
5. Use it in a sentence of your own.

### Word puzzles
A word puzzle makes vocabulary fun. A simple puzzle is a word search or a fill-in puzzle.
Here is a fill-in puzzle. Use the words: safe, cross, clear, traffic.
1. Wait until the road is ______.
2. The road has a lot of ______ at noon.
3. Look both ways before you ______.
4. Stay ______ near the road.
Answers: clear, traffic, cross, safe.

### Matching game
Write words on one set of cards and meanings on another. Turn them face down. Turn over one of each. If they match, keep them. In pairs, play this with your own vocabulary words in English and in your mother tongue.

## PAGE 4: Inferring meaning from context
Inferring means working out something that is not stated directly. When you infer the meaning of a word, you use clues around it, called context.

### An example
"The road was so congested that the matatu could not move. Cars, lorries and motorbikes stood bumper to bumper."
What does congested mean? The clues are: the matatu could not move, and many vehicles stood close together. So congested means very crowded or full.

### Steps to infer meaning
1. Read the sentence with the unknown word.
2. Look for clues in the words near it.
3. Ask what makes sense in that place.
4. Make a guess. Replace the word with your guess and see if the sentence still makes sense.
5. Check in a simple dictionary.

### Another example
"The nurse bandaged the wound gently. The blood stopped and the child felt better."
Clues: the nurse, blood, the child felt better. A wound is an injury on the body.

### Dictionaries
A dictionary lists words in alphabetical order. Find the first letter, then the second, and so on. Some languages have a simple dictionary made for schools. Ask your teacher if one is available in your language.

## PAGE 5: Role playing a dialogue on safety
Now put your reading into action. In groups of three or four, create and perform a role play about personal safety.

### Ideas for topics
• Crossing the road.
• A stranger offers you a lift.
• Something is burning in the kitchen.
• A friend is hurt in the playground.

### Model role play: a stranger
Child: Good afternoon.
Stranger: Hello, child. Do you want a ride home?
Child: No, thank you. I will wait for my mother.
Stranger: Are you sure? It is a long way.
Child: Yes, I am sure. I am going to tell my teacher.
Teacher: Is everything all right?
Child: A stranger asked me to go with him. I said no.
Teacher: You did the right thing.

### Steps for role play
1. Plan the story and the characters.
2. Write a short script.
3. Practise reading it with the right voice.
4. Perform for the class.
5. The audience says what they enjoyed and what to improve.

Perform it in your mother tongue with help from an elder if you need it.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
A dialogue is a conversation shown with the speakers' names. We read it with expression, in turns, watching the punctuation. We understand new words from context, matching games and dictionaries. Inferring means using clues to work out meaning.

### Remember
• Read for meaning first.
• Use the clues around a word.
• Check with a dictionary.
• Look back at the text when answering questions.

### Common mistakes
• Reading the speaker's name aloud when acting a dialogue. The name only shows who speaks.
• Guessing a word without looking at the clues.
• Rushing to the next line without waiting your turn.
• Ignoring question marks and exclamation marks.

### Final activity
Write a four line dialogue between two friends about staying safe. Swap with a partner, read it aloud, and underline one new word each. Work out the meaning of the word from context.

## QUIZ
1. What is a dialogue?
   A) A conversation between two or more people
   B) A long poem
   C) A list of words
   D) A kind of map
   Answer: A
   Why: A dialogue is a conversation between two or more speakers.
2. In a written dialogue, what usually comes before each spoken line?
   A) The name of the speaker
   B) A picture
   C) The date
   D) A number
   Answer: A
   Why: The speaker's name shows who is talking.
3. What does inferring the meaning of a word mean?
   A) Copying the word
   B) Working out its meaning using clues in the text
   C) Ignoring the word
   D) Spelling the word backwards
   Answer: B
   Why: We use clues in the context to work out what a word means.
4. In the sentence: The road was so congested that the matatu could not move. What does congested mean?
   A) Empty
   B) Very crowded
   C) Very clean
   D) New
   Answer: B
   Why: The matatu could not move because there were too many vehicles.
5. Fill in the missing word: Wait until the road is ______ before you cross. (2 marks)
   Answer: clear
   Why: One mark for the word clear or a similar correct word such as empty, and one mark for correct spelling.
6. State three steps for reading your part in a dialogue. (3 marks)
   Answer: Read silently first, underline your lines and practise them, watch for the punctuation, and wait for your cue.
   Why: One mark for each valid step.
7. Explain how you can find the meaning of a new word in a text. (3 marks)
   Answer: Read the sentence, look for clues near the word, guess and test the guess, then check in a dictionary.
   Why: One mark each for clues, testing a guess, and checking in a dictionary.
8. Write a short two line dialogue between a child and a teacher about crossing the road. (2 marks)
   Answer: Child: Teacher, is it safe to cross now? Teacher: Look right, left and right again first.
   Why: One mark for the speaker names with colons and one for suitable content.
