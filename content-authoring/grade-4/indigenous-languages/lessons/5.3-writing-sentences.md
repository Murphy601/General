## PAGE 1: What makes a sentence?
A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense. It says something about someone or something. A sentence is not just any group of words.

"The trader sells maize." is a sentence. It tells us who and what happens.
"The trader in the market" is not a sentence. It does not tell us what the trader does.

### Two main parts
Every complete sentence has two main parts.
1. The subject: who or what the sentence is about.
2. The predicate: what the subject does or is. The predicate always includes a verb.

### Examples
The trader | sells maize.
My sister | counts the money.
The customers | are waiting.

### Key question
How do we connect ideas in sentences? We use joining words, called conjunctions, and we build sentences from clear parts.

### Try it
Look at three sentences in your exercise book. Draw a line between the subject and the predicate in each.

### How to find the subject
Ask: who or what is doing the action? In "The seller counts the money", the seller is doing the action, so the subject is the seller.

### How to find the predicate
Ask: what is said about the subject? Counts the money.

### Practice
Divide these sentences into subject and predicate.
1. Mama Neema sells fruit.
2. The buyers pay in shillings.
3. My brother saves coins.
Answers: Mama Neema | sells fruit. The buyers | pay in shillings. My brother | saves coins.

### In your mother tongue
Take three short sentences in your language. Find who or what the sentence is about, and what is said about it. In some languages the order of the parts is different from English, and that is fine.

## PAGE 3: Building sentences from jumbled words
Sometimes words are mixed up, and we put them in order to make a sentence.

### Example
Jumbled: shillings the pays customer fifty
Correct: The customer pays fifty shillings.

### Steps
1. Find the verb. It is pays.
2. Find who does it: the customer.
3. Place the subject first, then the verb, then the rest.
4. Add a capital letter and full stop.

### Practise
Reorder:
1. buys money the with boy sweets
2. seller runs in a shop the
3. the coins counts girl the
Answers:
1. The boy buys sweets with money.
2. The seller runs in a shop.
3. The girl counts the coins.

Some jumbled sentences may have more than one correct answer. Check that your sentence makes sense.

### Activity
Write a short sentence on money on a strip of paper. Cut it into words. Give the cut words to a partner to re-order.

## PAGE 4: Simple sentences and joining words
A simple sentence has one subject and one predicate. "I buy bread." Sometimes we want to say two things at once. We use a conjunction, a joining word, to join two simple sentences into a compound sentence.

### Common joining words
• and: adds an idea
• but: shows a difference
• or: shows a choice
• so: shows a result

### Examples
"I have money." + "I want a book." = "I have money, and I want a book."
"She wanted mangoes." + "They were too costly." = "She wanted mangoes, but they were too costly."
"You can pay now." + "You can pay later." = "You can pay now, or you can pay later."
"It was late." + "The shop closed." = "It was late, so the shop closed."

### Rule
Put a comma before the joining word when it joins two full sentences. Each half must have its own subject and verb.

### Practice
Join with and, but, or or so.
1. The trader was tired. He kept working. (but)
Answer: The trader was tired, but he kept working.
2. I saved fifty shillings. I bought a pen. (so)
Answer: I saved fifty shillings, so I bought a pen.

## PAGE 5: Vocabulary bank and compound sentences
Learning new words helps you write richer sentences. This theme is money. New words may include: price, change, receipt, saving, bargain, debt, wages.

### Building a word bank
1. Write each new word on a flashcard.
2. Add a picture or a meaning on the back.
3. Display the cards in class.
4. Add the word in your mother tongue beside the English word, after asking your teacher or an elder.

### Meanings
• price: what something costs
• change: money returned after paying more than the cost
• receipt: a paper that shows you paid
• saving: money kept for later
• bargain: to discuss the price

### Write compound sentences
Use words from the bank.
"The price was too high, so I bargained."
"I gave the seller a hundred shillings, and she gave me change."
"I wanted to spend my money, but I decided to save it."

### Digital game
If your school has computers, play a drag and drop game where you join parts of sentences with and, but, or or. Without computers, use cards.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
A sentence has a subject and a predicate. It begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop. Two simple sentences can be joined with a conjunction to make a compound sentence, with a comma before the joining word.

### Remember
• Subject: who or what.
• Predicate: what it does or is.
• Conjunctions: and, but, or, so.
• Comma before the joining word in a compound sentence.

### Common mistakes
• Writing a group of words with no verb and calling it a sentence.
• Joining two sentences with only a comma and no joining word.
• Using but when the ideas do not contrast.
• Forgetting capital letters and full stops.

### Final activity
Write three simple sentences about a market. Then make two compound sentences from them. Read them aloud to your partner. Ask them to point out the joining word in each.

## QUIZ
1. What are the two main parts of a sentence?
   A) Subject and predicate
   B) Noun and pronoun
   C) Comma and full stop
   D) Capital and small letter
   Answer: A
   Why: A sentence has a subject and a predicate.
2. Which group of words is a complete sentence?
   A) The seller in the market
   B) Counts the money
   C) The seller counts the money.
   D) Of the market
   Answer: C
   Why: It has a subject, the seller, and a predicate with a verb.
3. Which word joins two ideas to show a difference?
   A) and
   B) but
   C) so
   D) or
   Answer: B
   Why: But shows contrast.
4. Which is a compound sentence?
   A) I like mangoes.
   B) The trader smiles.
   C) I have money, and I want a book.
   D) The big red bus.
   Answer: C
   Why: It joins two simple sentences with the word and.
5. Divide into subject and predicate: My brother saves coins. (2 marks)
   Answer: Subject: My brother. Predicate: saves coins.
   Why: One mark for each part.
6. Join with so: It was late. The shop closed. (2 marks)
   Answer: It was late, so the shop closed.
   Why: One mark for using so and one for the comma.
7. Put in order to make a sentence: shillings the pays customer fifty (2 marks)
   Answer: The customer pays fifty shillings.
   Why: One mark for the correct order and one for the capital and full stop.
8. Write a compound sentence about money using the word but. (3 marks)
   Answer: I wanted to buy sweets, but I decided to save my money.
   Why: One mark for two complete ideas, one for using but correctly, one for punctuation.
