## PAGE 1: Why stories matter
Stories are important in every community. Grandparents tell them to teach children. Elders tell them to remember the past. Storytellers use them to entertain. A good story can make us laugh, feel sad, feel brave or think about how to live well.

Creative writing means using your imagination to write stories, poems and other pieces. When you write creatively, you make something new with words.

### Key questions
• Why are stories important in communities? They teach values, preserve history, entertain and bring people together.
• What is the importance of artistic work in expressing creativity? Pictures, drawings, paintings and carvings can show ideas that words then explain. They can also give us ideas for stories.

### Stories about animals
In many communities, stories about animals teach about how to live with each other. In your own community, there may be stories in which animals are kind or unkind, clever or foolish. Ask an elder to share one. As you listen, think about the lesson the story teaches about caring for animals.

### Try it
Tell your partner in two sentences about a story you have heard that has an animal in it.

### What each element means
1. Title: the name of the story.
2. Characters: the people or animals in the story.
3. Setting: where and when the story happens.
4. Problem: the trouble or challenge the characters face.
5. Events: what happens, step by step.
6. Ending: how the problem is solved, and what we learn.

### Discuss in groups
Think of a story you know. Name its characters, setting and problem. Compare your ideas with another group.

## PAGE 3: Studying a picture for story ideas
A picture can hold a whole story. When you look at a picture carefully, you can see who is there, where they are, what they are doing and what might happen next.

### Steps for picture reading
1. Look at the whole picture first.
2. Look closely at each part: people, animals, objects, weather.
3. Ask: who, where, when, what, why?
4. Imagine what happened before the scene.
5. Imagine what will happen next.
6. Think of names for the characters.

### Practise with a described picture
Picture: A girl stands beside a thin dog under a tree. A bowl is empty on the ground. Dark clouds are in the sky. The girl holds a small bag.
Who: a girl and a dog.
Where: under a tree.
What: the bowl is empty, and the dog looks hungry.
Idea: She has brought food in her bag. It is about to rain, so she must hurry.

### Your own pictures
Bring a picture from a newspaper, a magazine, or draw one of an animal. In pairs, look at it and answer the five questions above.

## PAGE 4: Writing a story from the picture
Now turn your ideas into a story.

### Plan
Title: Neema and the Hungry Dog
Characters: Neema, a thin dog.
Setting: Under a tree, on a cloudy afternoon.
Problem: The dog is hungry and rain is coming.
Events: Neema gives food, takes the dog home, makes a dry shelter.
Ending: The dog is safe. Neema learns to care for animals.

### Model story
"Neema and the Hungry Dog
One cloudy afternoon, Neema walked home from school. Under a big tree, she saw a thin dog beside an empty bowl. The dog looked at her with sad eyes. Neema opened her bag and shared her bread. The dog ate it quickly. Then thunder rumbled. Neema knew the rain was coming. She ran home with the dog behind her. Her father helped her make a dry shelter with old sacks. That night the dog slept safely. Neema smiled, because she had learned that a small kindness can save a life."

### Writing tips
• Begin with a sentence that makes the reader curious.
• Use the past tense all the way through.
• Describe what characters see and feel.
• Use paragraphs.
• End with the lesson.

## PAGE 5: Peer review and picture stories
After writing, check your story and share it.

### Peer review
Swap your story with a partner. Read it and use this checklist.
• Is there a clear title?
• Are the characters and setting clear?
• Is there a problem and a solution?
• Do the events follow in order?
• Are capital letters and full stops used correctly?
• What did I like best?
Write one comment and one suggestion.

### Write in your mother tongue
Once your idea is ready, write it in your mother tongue if you can. Ask your teacher or an elder to look at your writing to check the spelling of words. Do not worry about making mistakes. Correcting them is part of learning.

### Picture stories
You can also tell a story only with drawings. Draw four or five pictures in a row, showing the beginning, the middle and the end. Write a short sentence under each picture. Your drawings should show the same character throughout.

### Class gallery
Display picture stories on the wall. Walk around, look at each, and read them. Write a kind comment on a sticky note for each writer. Celebrate everyone's achievement.

## PAGE 6: Summary and common mistakes
A story has a title, characters, setting, a problem, events and an ending. A picture can give ideas for a story. We plan, write, review and share our stories.

### Remember
• Plan before you write.
• Look closely at a picture and ask who, where, what, why.
• Put events in order.
• End with a solution or lesson.

### Common mistakes
• Starting to write without a plan.
• A story with no problem, so nothing happens.
• Changing a character's name by accident.
• Forgetting to end the story.
• Copying a story instead of using your own imagination.

### Final activity
Choose a picture of an animal. In five sentences, write a story that has a problem and a happy ending. Read it aloud. Ask your partner to name the characters, setting and problem they heard.

## QUIZ
1. Which element tells where and when a story happens?
   A) Setting
   B) Title
   C) Ending
   D) Character
   Answer: A
   Why: The setting is the place and time of the story.
2. What is the problem in a story?
   A) The trouble or challenge the characters face
   B) The title
   C) The last word
   D) A drawing
   Answer: A
   Why: Most stories are built around a problem that has to be solved.
3. Which of these is a character?
   A) Neema
   B) Under a tree
   C) Cloudy afternoon
   D) The ending
   Answer: A
   Why: Characters are the people or animals in a story.
4. What should you do first when you study a picture for a story?
   A) Look at the whole picture
   B) Write the ending
   C) Cover it
   D) Copy another story
   Answer: A
   Why: You begin by seeing the whole picture before looking at details.
5. Name the six elements of a story. (3 marks)
   Answer: Title, characters, setting, problem, events and ending.
   Why: Award 3 marks for all six, 2 marks for four or five, and 1 mark for two or three.
6. Give two reasons why stories are important in communities. (2 marks)
   Answer: They teach values and lessons, they preserve history and culture, or they entertain.
   Why: One mark for each valid reason.
7. A picture shows a boy standing beside a lost goat near a river. Write two questions to help you plan a story. (2 marks)
   Answer: Who is the boy and where did the goat come from? What will the boy do next?
   Why: One mark for each relevant question.
8. Write a three-sentence story beginning from the picture in question 7. (3 marks)
   Answer: Any three sentences with a clear character, a problem (the lost goat) and a step towards a solution, such as: Juma found a lost goat beside the river. He asked people nearby who owned it. Soon its owner arrived and thanked him.
   Why: One mark for a character and setting, one for a problem, one for a solution or ending.
